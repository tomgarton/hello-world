# Stillness, not flatness, carries Puggle forward

The next layer of refinement for Puggle is mostly restraint written down as rules. Every screen version of Miffy for which there is evidence kept solid colour and a nearly motionless face; what the adaptations gave up was the flatness of the page and, later, the characters' silence. The stop-motion series (2003) and film (2013) turned her into a three-dimensional puppet, and the first CGI series (2015) was deliberately animated in a slightly jerky, held-frame way so it would feel like those puppets instead of slick computer animation. The classical sources agree with that direction: Disney's and Pixar's own teaching says life comes from timing, a visible thought and clear staging, not facial detail, and Pixar found on *Up* that very simple characters look more alive when animated less. For Puggle that means two jobs. On design, turn the look into a model sheet of countable constants and a "never" list, and prove the cast reads as black silhouettes. On animation, sequence what the rig already does: blink for a reason, let the eyes lead the head, change expression only while the body is still, delay child parts by a frame or two, and let only one character move at a time. Almost all of this is cheap in a part-based spring rig because it is parameters and ordering, not new artwork; the costly items are true joint actions such as holding hands or passing a ball. Several details people repeat about Miffy on screen (how the outline was handled, whether her mouth moves in speech, whether she blinks) could not be verified from any source and are flagged below.

## Miffy kept a still face for thirty years and gave up the flat page

### What the books fix

Dick Bruna painted Miffy's contour by hand in black poster paint over a traced pencil drawing, filled it with flat colour from a small fixed palette, used no perspective, and built the face from two dots and a small cross looking straight at the reader ([Thames & Hudson](https://www.thamesandhudson.com/blogs/all-news-features/the-making-of-an-illustrator-dick-bruna-s-miffy-1)). Emotion came from very small changes, a slight head tilt or a blink of the eye, reached by discarding hundreds of attempts ([Thames & Hudson](https://www.thamesandhudson.com/blogs/all-news-features/the-making-of-an-illustrator-dick-bruna-s-miffy-1)). Because the face is nearly blank, small shifts in alignment change the feeling, and the reader fills in the rest ([The New World](https://www.thenewworld.co.uk/dale-shaw-rampant-rabbit-the-simple-joy-and-complex-roots-of-dick-brunas-miffy/)). The design itself drifted for decades in one direction: the 1955 Miffy had floppy ears and did not meet the reader's eye; later versions grew **a rounder head, more symmetrical ears, and bigger, wider-set eyes** ([Thames & Hudson](https://www.thamesandhudson.com/blogs/all-news-features/the-making-of-an-illustrator-dick-bruna-s-miffy-1)). Those are the same levers available to a platypus toddler.

### What each screen version kept and changed

| Version | Technique | Kept | Adapted or given up |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1992, Gene Deitch | Drawn, flat cels, five-minute storybook episodes | Frontal face, no head turns, no profile, Bruna's own flat colours, storyboards approved by Bruna | Almost nothing; flat colour showed dust and dulled under stacked cels |
| 2003, *Miffy and Friends* | Stop-motion puppets (Pedri Animation) | Characters do not speak; one narrator; rounded forms; simple colour | Flatness: a critic calls it three-dimensional, not flat like the books |
| 2013, *Miffy the Movie* | Stop-motion puppets with digital backgrounds | Near-static faces; colour fidelity; recognisable simple figures | Characters have voices, played over faces that barely move |
| 2015, *Miffy's Adventures Big and Small* | 3D CGI (Blue-Zoo, Maya) | The puppet series' feel, by holding each image for two or three frames | Full voice cast plus narrator; seven-minute stories |
| 2025, *Miffy* | 3D CGI (Superprod) | Makers say the same iconic look | New sibling, contemporary setting, more complex personality |

The strictest rules on record are Deitch's, for the 1992 drawn series. Characters were always seen face-on, looking at the child, and in his words "they must never turn their heads or be seen in profile!"; Bruna wanted design, colour and animation to match the books with no embellishment, in pure flat colour, and Deitch took storyboards to Utrecht in batches for approval ([Gene Deitch](https://genedeitchcredits.wordpress.com/roll-the-credits/49-dick-bruna/)). That is the closest precedent for Puggle's flat rig.

The 2003 stop-motion series made its decisive choice in structure, not looks. The characters never speak and a narrator explains what they do ([Wikipedia: Miffy and Friends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy_and_Friends)), so a face with a fixed cross for a mouth never has to form words. Pedri Animation's project manager described the work as a pure commission, shot at about **five minutes of animation a week** with five permanent staff plus about fifteen more ([AV & Entertainment, 2006](https://av-entertainment.nl/lees-artikel/382/Stop-motion-animatie-volop-in-beweging)). A Dutch reviewer later noted the cost: the series is three-dimensional, not flat as in the books ([FilmTotaal](https://www.filmtotaal.nl/recensie/9847)).

The 2013 film shows the limit case. It is puppet stop-motion with digital backgrounds and a voice cast, yet the faces barely move: the reviewer reports that only one character's mouth (Grunty's) moves, and only to show emotion; he adds that adults may find the static faces odd but the young audience probably will not notice ([FilmTotaal](https://www.filmtotaal.nl/recensie/9847)). The same review notes that the song animation is simply repeated, which is reuse treated as normal.

For the 2015 CGI series the one hard technique fact comes second-hand, from a blogger's notes on a talk by Blue-Zoo co-founder Tom Box: the show was animated in Maya but **on twos and threes**, meaning each image is held for two or three frames so motion looks slightly stepped, to make it feel like stop-motion ([Animation Apprentice](https://animationapprentice.blogspot.com/2015/11/blue-zoo-explains-challenges-of-running.html)). So the CGI imitates the puppets, not the page. The series has a voice cast as well as a narrator ([Wikipedia: Miffy's Adventures Big and Small](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy%27s_Adventures_Big_and_Small)). For the 2025 series only announcements exist: the makers say the look stays while personality, family and setting change ([AWN](https://www.awn.com/news/studiocanal-mercis-superprod-team-miffy-3dcg-preschool-series)).

### What that teaches about a flat, frontal, no-mouth character

Three conclusions follow, and the third is a judgement from the pattern, not something a maker states. First, **the still face is what every version protected**; flatness was traded away the moment puppets were used, and nobody treated that as fatal. Puggle has no reason to trade it, since her rig is flat, so she can keep both. Second, the cheapest answer to "what does the mouth do" is nothing: keep the character silent and let narration, sound and the body carry meaning. Hello Kitty is the warning in the other direction. Her designers left the mouth off so viewers could project their own feelings ([Tofugu](https://www.tofugu.com/japan/hello-kitty-face/)), but once she had to carry dialogue in animation she was given a visible mouth, and analysts saw the missing mouth as a handicap for television ([Wikipedia: Hello Kitty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hello_Kitty)). Puggle should never be asked to lip-sync. Third, smooth computer motion is the enemy of this look. Blue-Zoo fought it by stepping frames; the equivalent in a spring rig is quick moves between clearly different poses followed by real holds, which the rig already favours.

## Classical animation says life comes from timing and thought, not facial detail

The twelve Disney principles were set down by Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston in 1981 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation)). The most useful restatement for Puggle is John Lasseter's 1987 paper, because it applies them to Luxo Jr., a hinged desk lamp with no face at all ([Lasseter 1987](https://www.ics.uci.edu/~fowlkes/class/ics162/exercises/lasseter.pdf)). Everything in this section comes from that paper unless another source is given.

**Timing** is how long an action takes; it tells the viewer weight and mood. Luxo Jr. hops fast with his head up and little time on the ground when happy; after the ball pops, each hop is slower, longer on the ground, head down. Same move, different feeling, changed only by numbers. Size sets tempo too: big characters start and stop slowly, small ones are quick. A classic table from Thomas and Johnston shows that the same head tilt reads as a crisp order at about four in-between frames, as trying to get a better look at seven, and as thoughtful appraisal at nine.

**Anticipation** is a small preparatory move in the opposite direction, such as a dip before a hop. It also tells the viewer where to look, and the better prepared the audience is, the faster the action itself can be. **Follow-through and overlapping action** mean parts do not start or stop together: one part leads, the rest trail and settle later, with offsets as small as one or two frames (about 40 to 80 milliseconds). The eyes usually lead the head. **Secondary action** is a smaller motion caused by or supporting the main one, such as a tail trailing a hop; it must stay subordinate. **Arcs** are curved paths; straight-line travel looks mechanical. **Squash and stretch** is flattening on impact and lengthening in fast travel, with one firm rule: volume stays constant, so a shape that gets shorter must get wider, otherwise it reads as shrinking. **Slow in and slow out** means easing gently out of one pose and into the next so the poses themselves are seen. Lasseter adds a software warning that applies directly to springs: Luxo Jr.'s base sank through the floor around each hop until the curve was locked, and a landing should hit the ground and stop, not ease through it.

**Staging** is presenting one idea so it is unmistakable. Only one idea should be visible at a time, and actions should be placed where they read as a silhouette, out to the side and not across the body. A change of expression made during a body move is simply not seen, so it belongs before or after the move; Ollie Johnston's notes say to hold the head still for expression changes and never to move anything without a purpose ([Ollie Johnston notes](https://eclass.aegean.gr/modules/document/file.php/511154/Theory/ollie_johnston_notes.pdf)). A **hold** is a pose kept on screen; a **moving hold** is a held pose kept faintly alive, which for Puggle is her breathing and blinking. Pixar's experience on *Up* is the important counterweight: the stylised Carl felt more alive when animated less, and on the simple design of Russell the smallest facial change made him look too old ([Computer Graphics World](https://cgw.com/Publications/CGW/2009/Volume-32-Issue-6-June-2009-/The-Shape-of-Animation.aspx)). Simple faces are fragile, and that fragility is the argument for a small fixed set of expressions.

**Appeal** means charm, simplicity and readability, not cuteness, and its commonest failure is "twins": both arms or both sides of the face doing the same thing at the same time, which looks wooden. A symmetrical, front-facing cut-out character produces twins by default.

On eyes, animation teachers agree that a blink is acting, not a timer. Characters blink on a change of thought, on a fast head move and on a change of eye direction; withholding blinks reads as intense attention and rapid blinks as shyness ([Shawn Kelly, Animation Mentor](https://newsletters.animationmentor.com/newsletter/1105/feature_tiptricks.html)). A standard blink is two frames closing, one closed and three opening at 24 frames a second, about a quarter of a second, always closing faster than it opens; a "thinking" blink is longer at about nine frames ([Bloop Animation](https://www.bloopanimation.com/blinking-animation/)). An eye dart, a quick jump of the eyes to a new target, takes two frames or so ([Spline Doctors](https://splinedoctors.blogspot.com/2006/09/eyes-in-animation.html)). None of these sources discusses dot eyes without lids, so translating them to Puggle is an inference.

For two characters, Luxo Jr. gives the clearest rule in the whole literature reviewed: **only one of the pair does an important action at any moment**, attention passes to whoever is moving more, and the first character looks toward where the second will appear so the audience is already looking there. The pair also differ in manner: Dad bats the ball with only his shade, Jr. throws his whole body at it. Lasseter staged their meeting in profile, which a frontal design cannot do; the substitute is discussed in the recommendations.

## Simple-design shows write rules as numbers and spend the savings on performance

The Simpsons' two founding design aims, according to Wikipedia's citation of Matt Groening's DVD commentaries, were recognisability in silhouette and faces simple enough that emotions are easy to change ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Simpsons_(franchise))). Consistency is then enforced by a style guide of nearly 500 pages whose surviving public pages specify countable facts: Lisa's hair is always eight points clustered in twos and threes, and her eyes are placed relative to her nose ([SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/573760/the-simpsons-style-guide/)). The lesson is to write rules as checkable numbers, not adjectives. In a code-driven rig those numbers are simply constants.

Bluey builds its whole world from one shape, a chunky rounded rectangle, and each new character is a new dog breed stylised into that same shape language ([Australian Cinematographer](https://acmag.com.au/2021/09/01/bluey/)). The characters were locked first and the world designed to suit them; the style bible asks for props that are cute, simplified and chunky, with doll's-house proportions, and warns against letting a character half-cover the prop a scene is about ([It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/features/how-we-built-bluey-s-world-cartoon-background-scenery-art-director-catriona-drummond-animation-090725)). A games team that rebuilt the look with the studio reports that outlines keep **a constant thickness regardless of distance from the camera** ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/bluey-s-simple-2d-look-required-gameloft-s-most-technically-challenging-art-pipeline)). The show stages characters mostly on one plane on a wide flat floor and cuts slowly so young viewers can keep track ([Australian Cinematographer](https://acmag.com.au/2021/09/01/bluey/)). One honest conflict: Bluey uses straight-on views sparingly, preferring angles in between, whereas Miffy is always frontal. Puggle should stay frontal, because the direct gaze is the heart of the Bruna idea and a flat rig has no convincing in-between angles.

Hey Duggee's creator stripped detail because six characters often share a shot and detail makes things congested for children, says reuse is not a dirty word, and notes that Duggee, the most detailed character, is hardest to animate because small shifts in facial features push him off-model, meaning away from his correct design ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/?p=149069)). Pocoyo gives each character one dominant colour, an accessory, and a signature sound and dance, and its early episodes move pose to pose, from one strong held position to the next ([Wikipedia: Pocoyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pocoyo)). Peppa Pig's crew change a character's arm colour so thin limbs stay visible on certain backgrounds ([Skwigly](https://www.skwigly.co.uk/peppa-pig-celaction-2d-and-the-future-of-british-animation/)). Sarah & Duck uses strong colours such as red only as accents ([Skwigly](https://skwigly.co.uk/sarah-duck)). For cut-out rigs specifically, one practitioner advises designing joints as circles so rotating parts hide their seams, and simplifying the design as if every frame were hand-drawn ([Motionographer](https://motionographer.com/2023/03/21/7-ways-to-mix-frame-by-frame-and-rigged-animation)).

For cute proportion, Preston Blair's formula as quoted by Cartoon Brew is a head large for the body, eyes low, wide and far apart, short fat legs tapering to small feet, and a bulging tummy ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/ideas-commentary/why-is-it-so-difficult-to-make-cute-characters-81160.html)); the scientific "baby schema" adds a bulging cheek region ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328x/14/1/68)), which supports cheeks as one of Puggle's emotion channels. Lasseter made Luxo Jr. read as a child by scaling parts unevenly: the bulb stays adult size while the shade shrinks and the rods shorten but keep their thickness ([Lasseter 1987](https://www.ics.uci.edu/~fowlkes/class/ics162/exercises/lasseter.pdf)). *Up* does the opposite job, separating characters by base shape, square Carl against round Ellie and Russell ([Computer Graphics World](https://cgw.com/Publications/CGW/2009/Volume-32-Issue-6-June-2009-/The-Shape-of-Animation.aspx)). Together those give a family-versus-friends method.

## App characters stay alive with a tiny idle vocabulary that never blocks the user

Duolingo's lesson characters idle with only three things: head nods, blinks and eyebrow movement ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/world-character-visemes)). Its character Lily avoids a visible loop by combining eight short head animations with eight short body animations, chosen independently, for more than 64 neutral variations, and builds expressions by layering eyes, brows and mouth separately; because she is deadpan, wide eyes made her read as too cheerful, so the team toned movement down, and waits are covered by a head tilt or a "ponder" ([Rive blog](https://rive.app/blog/duolingo-s-ai-powered-video-call-brings-lily-to-life)). The character stops speaking the moment the learner finishes ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/world-character-visemes)): the character never makes the user wait. Each Duolingo character also has its own animation for a correct answer, so the cast is told apart by movement ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/building-character/)).

On timing, interface guidance converges on 100 to 500 milliseconds, with the rule that the more often an animation is seen the shorter and subtler it should be ([Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/)). Apple describes a spring by duration and bounce: bounce 0 is the versatile default, about 0.15 is brisk without visible bounce, 0.3 is noticeably bouncy, and above about 0.4 is usually too much; springs also carry their speed smoothly when interrupted mid-move ([Apple WWDC23](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/)). Spine's documentation blends into an action faster than out of it in its example (0.2 seconds in, 0.4 out) and layers animations so a reaction changes only the parts it needs while the base loop continues underneath ([Spine](https://esotericsoftware.com/spine-applying-animations)). One vendor blog claims an unvaried idle loop becomes noticeable after about 90 seconds and recommends two to four extra idle moves at random intervals, though it gives no evidence for the figure ([MoCap Online](https://mocaponline.com/blogs/mocap-news/idle-animation-loop)).

For a tired parent at night, the relevant model is Finch, whose pet has a life of its own and sleeps at night, with a widget an industry analyst describes as anti-nag ([Deconstructor of Fun](https://www.deconstructoroffun.com/blog/x0hd2ssr80y5n7gv0w967pg7hwd7tl)). No source describes night-time animation rules for any app, so the night recommendations below are inference. Finally, Apple's Reduce Motion guidance says decorative motion stops when the setting is on, meaningful motion is replaced with a dissolve or colour change, and any ongoing motion should stop or have a control; scaling is on its list of common triggers ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-app-accessibility/reduced-motion-evaluation-criteria)). A looping breath is ongoing motion and squash is scaling.

## What could not be verified

The Miffy evidence is thinner than its reputation suggests. **No source describes how any animated Miffy handled the black outline**, whether puppet faces were painted or swapped, how heads turn while the face stays frontal, whether she blinks, or whether her cross mouth moves during speech in the 2015 or 2025 series. Researchers' recollections that the puppets and CGI drop the outline for soft matte surfaces, and that the cross flexes in dialogue, are unconfirmed and should be checked by watching episodes. The Blue-Zoo "twos and threes" fact is a blogger's notes from a talk, not a transcript. The widely repeated lines that Bruna wanted the line to have "a heartbeat", chose a brush to make it tremble, and moved features by fractions of a millimetre could not be confirmed because miffy.com's page on the black lines would not open. Bruna's "always facing you" and "never in profile" statements were seen only in search summaries; the firm source for the frontal rule is Deitch. Credits for the 2003 series conflict between sources, the puppet shoot location for the 2013 film is uncertain, and everything about the 2025 series comes from announcements read as summaries. A distributor catalogue lists 2005 and 2007 Miffy specials as CGI, which conflicts with 2015 being called her first CGI series; this is unresolved. No Mercis style guide was found.

On the classical side, *The Illusion of Life*, Richard Williams's *Animator's Survival Kit* and Preston Blair's books were not read directly; their content arrives through Lasseter, Wikipedia and Cartoon Brew. No verified head-to-body ratio for cute characters was found, nor any sourced rule for "line of action", line-up sheets, eye lines between characters, or how much a listening character may move. Shape-meaning conventions (circle friendly, square sturdy, triangle sharp) rest on Bluey's art director and design blogs, not Disney sources. No Groening first-hand quote on silhouette was retrieved, and the Simpsons style guide is public only as isolated pages. Bluey's own behind-the-scenes page and any account of how its characters were designed or acted could not be read.

For apps, no source gives breathing rates, idle amplitude limits, tested spring values for tails or accessories, blink intervals for a calm character, delays between one character's action and another's reply, or any night-mode animation rules. Squash percentages (about 5 per cent for restrained contexts) and anticipation lengths come from a single community page seen only in summary. Apple's Human Interface Guidelines motion page could not be read, and the accessibility flashing limits were not checked. All frame-to-millisecond conversions assume 24 frames a second, which the sources imply but do not always state. Every recommendation below marked "inference" is reasoning from these sources, not a rule any of them states.

## Design recommendations, in priority order

"Cheap" means it can be done by changing constants, rules or checks in the existing rig or its documentation; "moderate" means some new or revised artwork; "costly" means new parts, poses or systems.

| Priority | Recommendation | Basis | Cost in the rig |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Write the model sheet as numbers.** For each character record head width and height, eye spacing and eye height as fractions of head width, eye dot size, cheek position, brow rest position and travel limits, bill size and tilt limit, outline weight, body and limb lengths in head units, pivot positions, and accessory placement. One page per character plus one cast page. | Simpsons style guide; Duggee and Russell going off-model from tiny shifts | Cheap: these are the rig's constants, exported to a sheet |
| 2 | **Add a "never" list.** Never a mouth; never crossed eyes; never a profile or smooth head turn; outline weight never varies; head never squashes; brows never appear without a feeling; no more than one accessory each; no outline on backgrounds. | Simpsons guide's forbidden-drawings idea (summary only); Deitch's Miffy rules | Cheap |
| 3 | **Run the silhouette test on the cast and on the moves.** Fill every character solid black at thumbnail size; each must be identifiable by bill, ears, snout, spines or tail plus accessory. Repeat for wave, hop and any prop pose: the gesture must sit outside the body shape. | Simpsons silhouette aim; Lasseter on staging in silhouette | Cheap to test; moderate where a character fails and needs redrawing |
| 4 | **Build the platypus family by proportion, not by redrawing.** Keep eyes and bill close to the same size across Pebble, Puggle, Mum, Dad, Nan and Pop; change body size and limb length; keep line weight constant. Fix heights on one line-up in head units, with tiers (baby, toddler, parent, grandparent) that still differ at thumbnail size. | Luxo Dad and Jr.; no sourced ratios exist, so tier sizes are the designer's call | Cheap if parts are shared; otherwise moderate |
| 5 | **Give each friend a different base shape inside one rounded family.** Suggested starting points, all inference: Kip the koala built on circles (round head, large round ears); Digby the wombat on a rounded square (low, wide, sturdy); Quill the echidna on a dome with spines as short rounded tufts, since points read as sharp; Sunny the quokka on an upright oval with small round ears. Check no two share both head shape and hue. | *Up*'s square-versus-round cast; Bluey's one shape family | Moderate: artwork review |
| 6 | **Check Puggle against the cute-proportion list.** Eyes low on the head and wide apart; short limbs tapering to small feet; slight tummy; cheeks as a distinct feature. Test that no expression state makes her look older than two and a half; lowered brows and narrowed eyes are the likely culprits (inference). | Blair via Cartoon Brew; baby schema; Russell on *Up* | Cheap: mostly constants |
| 7 | **Lock outline rules.** Constant weight on screen at every size and under any scaling; decide once whether overlapping parts show their own line or merge, and keep it; make joints circular so pivots hide seams; bake any hand-made wobble into the artwork and do not make the line shimmer frame to frame (the last point is inference). | Bluey via Gameloft; Motionographer | Cheap for weight and rules; moderate for joint artwork |
| 8 | **Define a small expression sheet.** A fixed set of eye, brow and cheek states (for example neutral, pleased, curious, sleepy, surprised, sad), each tested alone on the neutral face, with brows and eyes moving as a unit and cheeks rising toward the eyes for pleasure. Use one channel per beat; stack several only for the biggest moment. | Johnston on brows and eyes; Lasseter on cheeks; Bruna's one tear | Cheap: states and limits |
| 9 | **One owned colour and one accessory per character; quiet the world.** Keep backgrounds lower in contrast and saturation than the cast, reserve the strongest hue for characters and the one prop that matters, and check thin limbs against every background. Props should be chunky and echo the cast's shapes. | Pocoyo; Sarah & Duck; Peppa Pig; Bluey style bible | Cheap to check; moderate to repaint |

## Animation recommendations, in priority order

| Priority | Recommendation | Basis | Cost in the rig |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Stage expression changes in stillness.** Change eyes, brows or cheeks before or after a body move, never during; then hold. One idea at a time. | Lasseter; Johnston | Cheap: ordering |
| 2 | **Make blinks mean something.** Keep the irregular background blink, but also force a blink on every head tilt, every change of where she looks, and when something registers. Close faster than open: roughly 80 ms closing, 40 to 80 ms shut, 125 to 165 ms opening. Add a slower "thinking" blink of about 375 ms, and use it more at night. Hold back blinks for wonder; a quick double blink for shyness. A tiny brow dip with the blink helps. | Kelly; Bloop; Animation Apprentice; millisecond values converted from frame counts | Cheap |
| 3 | **Let the eyes lead.** Slide both eye dots a few pixels toward a target one or two frames before the head tilts that way, then let the head follow and settle. This is also how she "looks at" a cast member without turning. | Lasseter (eyes lead the head); Spline Doctors; dot sliding is inference | Cheap if eyes are separate parts |
| 4 | **Offset the parts.** Start head after body, bill and tail after head, hand after arm, accessory last, each about 40 to 80 ms late, and give loose parts softer springs so they settle later with one small overshoot. Never stop everything on the same frame; offset left and right slightly to break "twins". | Lasseter on overlap; spring softness values are untested suggestions | Cheap: delays and spring settings |
| 5 | **Add small anticipation.** A slight dip before a hop, a small counter-tilt before a tilt or nod, a glance before a wave. Keep it to a few per cent and a few tens of milliseconds; bigger only for rare, big moves. | Lasseter; magnitudes from a community page (unverified) | Cheap |
| 6 | **Fix the hop.** Travel on an arc, land hard on the ground line, then squash; do not let the spring ease into or through the floor. Squash the body only, about 5 per cent, widening as it shortens, pivoting at the feet; the head stays rigid. Vary by mood: happy is short on the ground with head up, tired is long on the ground with head down. | Lasseter; Johnston on skull size; squash figure unverified | Cheap to moderate: non-uniform scale distorts the outline, so the stroke must be redrawn at constant weight |
| 7 | **Give each character a movement signature.** Same verbs, different numbers: Pebble tiny and quick; Puggle quick and whole-bodied; Mum and Dad slower with smaller, head-led gestures; Nan and Pop slowest, barely bending; each friend with its own tempo and a favourite gesture. | Luxo Dad and Jr.; *Up*; Duolingo's unique animations | Cheap: per-character parameters |
| 8 | **One mover at a time.** Breathing and blinks are always allowed; any larger move needs a single scene-wide "turn". Give each character a different breathing period and blink timer so they never pulse together. A tapped character reacts at once; others glance toward it a beat later and stay smaller. | Lasseter's Luxo rule; the scheduler is inference | Cheap to moderate: a small scene scheduler |
| 9 | **Add look-and-answer pairs before any touching.** A looks at B (eye slide, blink, slight lean), a short pause, B looks back, B answers with a nod or wave in the gap between them, never across the body. Build a small pool of such pairs with no immediate repeats. | Luxo pre-directing the eye; rest is inference, no source gives rules | Moderate: scripted sequences from existing moves |
| 10 | **Vary the idle by combination.** Three or four short head moves and three or four body moves chosen independently, never the same twice running, at most one every few breaths; breathing applied on top of every pose so no reaction cancels it. Keep amplitude low enough that it never pulls the eye from the data. | Lily's 8 by 8; Spine layering; AnimSchool; 90-second figure unevidenced | Cheap to moderate |
| 11 | **Tune springs and blends for calm.** Bounce 0 to 0.15 for most motion, 0.2 to 0.3 only for daytime hops and waves; go into a reaction in about 0.2 s and back out in about 0.4 s. Frequent events (logging a feed) get the smallest reaction; rare ones the largest. A new tap replaces queued reactions; the app never waits for her. | Apple WWDC23; Spine example values; NN/g; Duolingo | Cheap |
| 12 | **Trust the hold.** After a move, hold the pose with breath and blink only. Do not add fidgeting to fill silence. | *Up*; Bruna's one image per thought | Cheap: remove motion |
| 13 | **Night and Reduce Motion states.** At night she is asleep or drowsy by default, and a tap earns a small stir, not the full wake (inference). | 14 | **True joint actions last.** Sitting together is the cheapest (new held poses, no contact). Holding hands needs a shared contact point both arms follow. ## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. The instinct with a simple character is to add: more moves, more expressions, more activity between cast members. The evidence points the other way. Miffy's guardians let flatness go before they let her face move; Pixar's animators found their simplest characters came alive when left alone in a pose; Duolingo runs a famous cast on nods, blinks and eyebrows. What separates a puppet from a character in all of these is order and reason: the glance before the tilt, the blink when something lands, the pause before another character answers. Puggle's rig already has the parts. The next layer is a written set of constants that keeps her on model and a set of sequencing rules that make each small move look like a thought.

The one piece of homework no document could do is to watch the footage. Half an hour comparing the 2003 stop-motion series, the Blue-Zoo series and the 2025 series, noting how long poses are held, whether and when Miffy blinks, how she looks at another character, and what the outline and mouth do, would settle most of the open Miffy questions above and would be the best possible reference for Puggle's look-and-answer pairs.
