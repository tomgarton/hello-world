# Part 1. The report

## One engine per book builds classics

The best-loved books for under-fives are each built on a single engine a child can predict by the second page-turn (a search, a count, a routine, a refrain, a game), poured into a fixed container that the series reuses, with one job for the child's hands or voice on every opening and a warm return to safety at the end. What suits each age follows a steady line that guidance bodies agree on: looking and listening in the first six months, handling and peek-a-boo to twelve months, naming and predicting from one to three, and following real stories with a twist from three to five. The evidence on reading aloud is real but modest (an average effect of about 0.19 in the strictest meta-analysis), with the clearest gains from back-and-forth talk with two- and three-year-olds and from re-reading the same book. On screens, the research draws one sharp line: animation and sound that carry the story help, while hotspots, games and anything that sits beside the story hurt, and an adult reading with the child beats every digital enhancement. For a platypus series inside a phone and tablet app, that points to a small set of repeatable story shapes matched to age, a lift-the-flap only where the flap is the story beat, an adult-as-reader default, and a positioning of age two to five, because Australian guidance recommends no screen time under two. The cast should be told apart by silhouette, one unshared prop or colour, a like or skill instead of a fault, exact ages and a sound or line a child can say; koala and wombat are crowded territory, while platypus, echidna and quokka are comparatively open. Much of the craft advice below is convention or inference, not research, and it is labelled as such throughout.

*How to read the labels.* **Research** means a peer-reviewed study or meta-analysis. **Guidance** means advice from a health, professional or charitable body, or a platform rule. **Convention** means a publishing or industry rule of thumb, or a maker's own stated practice. **Inference** means the researchers' or this report's reasoning from the evidence. "Snippet-level" means the researcher saw only a search-result summary of the source, not the page. "Unverified" means the point came from a researcher's memory and was not confirmed against any source; every such flag in the notes is kept here, and the unverified items are gathered in their own section near the end so they are not mistaken for findings.

### A fixed container and one visible engine underlie every classic

#### Miffy, Spot and the Caterpillar give the child one job per opening

**What the sources document.** Dick Bruna's Miffy books hold **twelve story pages, each with one picture and four lines of verse in which line two rhymes with line four**, and the plots are everyday experiences that always end happily ([Wikipedia: Miffy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy)). The official site describes a compact 16 cm square with the drawing on the right and the text on the left (snippet-level) ([miffy.com](https://www.miffy.com/his-books)). Bruna worked with a hand-brushed black line and a small fixed set of flat colours, said "No line is redundant", and found that the square, first imposed by paper costs, let children hold the book themselves ([DesignCurial](https://old.designcurial.com/news/miffy-creator-dick-bruna---the-art-of-reduction-270115-4497133/)). Sources disagree on the number of Miffy titles (30-plus, 32 or 33) and on whether the square began in 1959 or 1963, and Wikipedia's "ages four to eight" conflicts with how publishers position the books. Eric Hill's Where's Spot? (1980) grew from a flap on an advertising piece that delighted his two-year-old son; he built a seek-and-find story around the mechanism and made a handmade dummy before there was a manuscript ([Publishers Weekly](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/obituaries/article/62808-obituary-eric-hill.html)). The Very Hungry Caterpillar (1969) is a 32-page book that lays a count of one to five over the days of the week, breaks the pattern with Saturday's feast and a stomach ache, and resolves in a butterfly; the first version, about a worm, had no real ending until editor Ann Beneduce suggested a caterpillar ([Wikipedia: The Very Hungry Caterpillar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Very_Hungry_Caterpillar); [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind)). The die-cut holes exist so that a child can put a finger through the page ([Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind)). Two close relatives work the same way: Dear Zoo sends a run of animals, each unsuitable for one stated reason, until the right one arrives ([1001 Stories](https://1001stories.co.uk/stories/dear-zoo)), and Maisy walks through one first experience or outing per book ([Walker Books Australia](https://www.walkerbooks.com.au/book/9780763615031/)).

Sales figures for all of these are publisher or estate claims and conflict by source: Miffy more than 85 million (BBC, 2013) against "over 100 million" elsewhere; Spot more than 50 or 60 million; the Caterpillar more than 50 or 55 million. The gap on Dear Zoo, 13 million claimed against 2.5 million tracked by Nielsen (snippet-level), shows how soft the headline numbers are ([The Bookseller](https://thebookseller.com/features/macmillan-marks-dear-zoo-milestone)). No word count for any of these books was verified.

**Inference.** The shared skeleton is a one-opening set-up that states the want, a run of five to eight near-identical beats, one break in the pattern, and a short warm close that returns to home, food, parent or sleep. In flap books the unit is the flap, not the page: each opening holds a question and its answer, so there are two reveals per spread. The lead characters are deliberately thin, a child-proxy with a secure family and almost no backstory, and hard subjects are handled inside the normal format instead of a special one: Bruna took the same twelve calm beats to hospital and to a grandmother's death, in Dear Grandma Bunny, which won the Dutch Silver Slate in 1997 ([Wikipedia: Miffy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy)). In all three cases the book was designed as an object first and a manuscript second, and all three artists came from commercial graphic design, which explains the poster logic of one idea, flat colour and strong silhouette.

#### Donaldson, Hargreaves and Scarry build on the line, the character and the spread

**What the sources document.** The Gruffalo (1999) is 32 pages and about 700 words in rhyming couplets with a strong triple metre, deliberately broken at the moments of surprise; the mouse meets three predators, invents a monster for each, then meets the real monster and walks back past the same three in reverse ([Wikipedia: The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)). A catalogue gives 687 words for The Gruffalo and 812 for Zog; neither was counted independently, so "about 700" is the safer statement ([TeachingBooks](https://library.teachingbooks.net/tb.cgi?tid=9121)). Julia Donaldson came from songwriting, says good rhyme lets her be concise and bad rhyme makes a story drag, and holds that rhyme without rhythm is pointless ([Publishers Weekly Q&A](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/interviews/article/47027-q-a-with-julia-donaldson-and-axel-scheffler.html); [Tes](https://www.tes.com/news/get-kids-reading-secret-you-know-turn-terrible-gruffalo)). Axel Scheffler's creature was made rounder and less frightening at the editor's request, and since The Snail and the Whale he has hidden a small Gruffalo in almost every book with Donaldson ([Picturebook Makers](https://blog.picturebookmakers.com/post/axel-scheffler)). In Zog, an accident-prone dragon fails repeatedly at school and a girl appears each time to tend the injury ([Books for Keeps](https://booksforkeeps.co.uk/review/zog/)).

Each Mr. Men book is a 14 by 12.6 cm paperback with 16 to 18 pages of text facing 15 to 17 marker-pen pictures, built on one character with one dominant trait ([Wikipedia: Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men)). Roger Hargreaves was an advertising copywriter, and the rights-holder's current claim is more than 250 million copies; older sources give 100 million and 85 million, so the figure is a claim and not an audit ([New Statesman](https://www.newstatesman.com/culture/books/2021/08/fifty-years-of-mr-men-books)). The criticism is specific and peer-reviewed: an analysis of 47 Mr. Men and 34 Little Miss books found that Little Miss characters needed rescuing in 51.5% of stories against 32.6% for Mr. Men ([Yorkshire Post](https://www.pressreader.com/uk/yorkshire-post/20180508/281792809659729)), and the follow-up in Sex Roles found male characters more adventurous and female ones more domestic and passive, with trait names tracking gender stereotypes ([White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/207093/)).

Richard Scarry's books run long (Cars and Trucks and Things That Go is 72 pages) and mostly avoid plot; Chris Ware describes the method as "images-as-words", with labelled pictures organised by themed spread, animals doing human jobs, and a tiny gold bug hidden on nearly every page of Cars and Trucks so the child keeps looking ([Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry); [Penguin listing](https://www.penguin.com.au/books/cars-and-trucks-and-things-that-go-9780307157850)). Best Word Book Ever was later revised to fix its 1960s roles: a father added to the kitchen, job titles made gender-neutral, a menorah added to a holiday page ([Kottke](https://kottke.org/15/11/a-more-progressive-richard-scarry)).

**Inference.** The table below is the researchers' synthesis, not a sourced comparison.

| | Donaldson and Scheffler | Mr. Men and Little Miss | Scarry's Busytown |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core unit | The line and the refrain | The character | The spread |
| Extent | 32 pages, about 700 to 800 words | About 32 to 36 small pages of prose (word count unverified) | 72 to 96 large pages of labels and captions |
| Best age within 0 to 5 | 3 to 5, listenable from 2.5 | 3 to 5, then early solo reading | 1.5 to 5, the widest span |
| The adult's job | Perform the verse, hold the beat, pause for the rhyme | Tell it deadpan, like a joke | Talk, ask, name, follow the finger |
| The picture's job | Adds setting, comedy and detail beyond the text | Restates the sentence | Is the content |
| Re-read driver | Joining in, anticipating the twist | Collecting the set | Finding new details and the hidden bug |
| Main weakness | Bad verse is worse than prose | Flat labels, dated gender split | Needs time and an engaged adult; role stereotypes |

The Gruffalo's there-and-back mirror means almost nothing new has to be introduced after the midpoint. Zog makes failure safe because the child knows help is coming. The Mr. Men criticism is structural: when the name is the trait, the character cannot change, and a child may hear a trait name as a verdict. A cast defined by what characters like doing or are good at avoids this, and the "find the trait a use" ending is available without the correction endings critics dislike. The Scarry revisions show that a world built from jobs makes a statement with every label, so roles need a deliberate spread from the start.

#### Bluey swaps the curriculum for a game with an emotional snag

**What the sources document.** A Bluey episode runs seven minutes; Joe Brumm's process sometimes begins with notes on his own family's games and conflicts; the emotions inside play, such as jealousy and regret, drive the conflict; and there is no antagonist ([Wikipedia: Bluey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))). Brumm left out literacy and numeracy on purpose: "There's no counting in Bluey, there's no learning this or that ... just show 'em playing" ([Wikipedia: Bluey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))). His reported lines that the best episodes have "wild premises that follow rigid logic", and that the writers rarely use B stories, reached the researcher only through search summaries ([Ragan](https://ragan.com/?p=288582)). Both parents join the play and validate feelings, and the father commits to his game roles while visibly reluctant ([Wikipedia: List of Bluey characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_characters)). Developmental researchers cite the show as an example of children processing fears and feelings through pretend play, and note episodes in which grandparents are taught by the children ([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/bluey-teaches-children-and-parents-alike-about-how-play-supports-creativity/)).

The books prove the format transfers to print. Penguin Random House Australia reports **over 10 million Bluey books sold across 100 titles in Australia and New Zealand** by October 2025 ([Penguin Australia](https://www.penguin.com.au/news/5097-10-million-bluey-books-sold)). Episode adaptations are mainly 24-page board books, often with a novelty such as flaps or glow-in-the-dark, and 32-page hardbacks that keep the episode title ([Wikipedia: List of Bluey books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_books)). No word counts and no account of how an episode is cut down were found.

The other preschool series each keep one small everyday event in a fixed container. Peppa Pig runs about five minutes with a narrator who says "Oh, dear" when things go wrong ([Wikipedia: Peppa Pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peppa_Pig)). Hey Duggee keeps a deliberately conventional frame of a carer, a club and a few children so that the content inside can vary ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/?p=149069)). Sarah & Duck's narrator talks to a child and a duck who does not speak ([Wikipedia: Sarah & Duck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_%26_Duck)). Daniel Tiger is the one built on an explicit curriculum, delivered as a short sung "strategy song" that families report using in daily life ([Fred Rogers Productions](https://www.fredrogers.org/production/daniel-tigers-neighborhood/)).

**Inference.** The Bluey template is an ordinary setting, a game with a clear rule, an emotional snag inside the game, and a resolution supplied by the game's own logic. The dual audience is structural: the child follows the game while the adult follows the tired, committed parent inside it. A narrator is what lets very young or non-speaking characters carry a story, which matters for a toddler lead and a baby; Bluey manages without one only because its leads are older and verbal. Each show also has a fixed ritual ending, and a fixed last spread does the same job in a book series. The Maisy model, one first experience per book, is the smallest viable story unit for under-threes; the Bluey model suits three to five.

### From faces at twelve inches to plot twists at four

**Guidance.** The age progression is the best-agreed part of the whole field, though it rests on guidance, not trials. From birth to six months babies recognise familiar voices and suit rhymes, lullabies, large faces and high-contrast or cloth books ([AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/choosing-books-for-children-and-families/)); newborns see best at about twelve inches, so a page should hold one or two objects, plenty of empty space and very few words ([Nemours](https://www.nemours.org/reading-brightstart/articles-for-parents/how-to-select-books-for-different-ages.html)). From seven to twelve months babies handle books themselves and like sturdy board books, textures, photos of babies and flaps for peek-a-boo. From one to two, toddlers name objects, predict what comes next in repetitive text and begin to name feelings; they want everyday topics such as bedtime, and silly books. Around **30 months** children can often turn paper pages. From three to five, preschoolers follow more complex stories, remember refrains, fill in blanks and guess the next word, and want a basic plot that finishes in one sitting. Nemours adds a practical point for restless toddlers: if a story is too long, talk about the pictures instead.

**Research.** The one research-grade developmental detail in the notes concerns humour, and it is snippet-level. Two-year-olds rely mainly on object-based jokes, using things the wrong way, while three-year-olds move towards conceptual and wrong-name jokes ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/jokes-toddlers-make)); a parent-report survey of 671 children found mislabelling emerges around two ([University of Bristol](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2021/november/humour-study.html)). Two small lab findings cut against received wisdom about novelty formats. In a study of 32 two-year-olds using a commercial first-words book, only the group whose flaps were sealed learned the new word above chance (snippet-level) ([Royal Holloway](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/lift-the-flap-features-in-first-words-picture-books-impede-word-l)). Secondhand reports say cartoons reduced word learning against photographs at 15 and 18 months, and pop-up features reduced it at 20 months and 30 to 36 months; neither paper was read ([Royal Holloway slides](https://su.royalholloway.ac.uk/media/20868/amber%20muhinyi%20research%20and%20practice%20event%2022.06.22.pdf.pdf)).

**Convention.** Word and page counts are rules of thumb, and no publisher's own guideline was found. One author's "personal references" are under 50 words in an 8- to 24-page board book for ages nought to three, under 200 words in a 24- to 32-page board book for two to five, and 500 words or fewer in a 32-page picture book for four to eight ([Boyds Mills](https://boydsmills.org/2019/04/08/picture-book-word-count-and-other-rules-meant-to-be-broken/)). The 32-page standard is an artefact of offset printing in 8- and 16-page signatures ([Lerner Books](https://lernerbooks.blog/2018/01/picture-book-page-counts-demystified.html)).

**Inference.** Humour should track what children themselves produce: a hat on a foot for under-threes, wrong names from about two and a half. Art should carry the story alone for under-threes, so a spread still works when the text is skipped. Flaps belong in play books where the reveal is the content, and pure naming books are better left plain. An app is not bound by printing signatures, so its page count can follow the child's attention instead of the press sheet. The summary table below combines the three kinds of claim and keeps them in separate columns.

| Age | Guidance: what the child does and enjoys | Convention: format and length | Inference: what fits this series |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 to 6 months | Voices, faces, rhyme, lullabies; one or two objects per page | High-contrast, cloth or small board books; very few words | Adult-read only; a short rhythmic text and bold single images. Sits against Australian screen guidance if delivered on a device |
| 7 to 12 months | Handling, textures, peek-a-boo, familiar objects | Sturdy board books; flaps; under 50 words | Peek-a-boo reveals and one image per screen, with the adult holding the device |
| 1 to 2 years | Naming, predicting repeated text, daily routines, silliness | Board books of 8 to 24 pages; under 50 to 100 words | Routine walk-throughs, search-and-find, a chant with a question; physical jokes |
| 2 to 3 years | Predictable text, rhyme, joining in; paper pages from about 30 months | Board books of 24 to 32 pages; under 200 words | Flap stories where the flap answers the text; count and offer-and-reject patterns; busy scenes to point at |
| 3 to 5 years | Longer stories, refrains, prediction, a plot; friends, siblings, sharing | 32-page picture book; about 500 words, ceiling 600 to 800 | Three-encounter and try-fail-helper stories; games with an emotional snag; wrong-name jokes |

What the notes could not source matters as much. No attention-span figures by age were found, and the "two to three minutes per year of age" rule that circulates online was not verified and should not be presented as research. Nothing was sourced on typical fears by age, vocabulary milestones, or when children follow cause and effect. On themes, guidance bodies name the subjects (routines, goodbyes, feelings, a new sibling, friends) but nothing was found on how to treat them without preaching; the AAP's remark that books "spark conversations" is the nearest thing to a model.

### Reading aloud helps modestly; conversation and re-reading do the work

**Research.** The strictest recent meta-analysis, Noble and colleagues (2019), put the overall effect of shared book reading on language at **about 0.19**, smaller than earlier estimates, sensitive to the type of control group, and negligible in some designs (snippet-level) ([Noble et al. 2019](https://hira.hope.ac.uk/3756/1/Noble%20et%20al.%20%282019%29.pdf)). Dialogic reading, in which the adult prompts, evaluates, expands and repeats so that the child becomes the teller, showed a moderate effect on expressive vocabulary (d = 0.59 across nine studies and 322 children) in Mol and colleagues' 2008 meta-analysis, shrinking substantially for older children (snippet-level) ([Leiden University repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2899524/view)). The two results conflict, since dialogic technique was not a significant moderator in the 2019 analysis. The fair reading, which is the researcher's inference, is that interaction helps most with two- and three-year-olds and with expressive vocabulary, and that claims of large effects are not supported by the stricter trials.

Re-reading has cleaner support. Three-year-olds who heard the same story three times retained two new words a week later, while those who heard three different stories containing the same words did not (snippet-level) ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00017/pdf)). The proposed mechanism is that a familiar story frees attention for the new words. Picture-book language is also richer than everyday talk to children: a corpus of 100 picture books contained more unique word types than matched child-directed speech (snippet-level) ([UEA repository](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/69099/)). No study was found that isolates the effect of refrain, cumulative structure, onomatopoeia or rhyme on under-fives.

**Guidance.** The AAP's 2024 policy statement recommends shared reading from birth and frames it as relationship-building as much as literacy; if screen-based reading is used, it recommends back-and-forth interaction around it (snippet-level) ([AAP news release](https://aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/aap/2024/american-academy-of-pediatrics-promotes-shared-reading-starting-in-infancy-as-a-positive-parenting-practice-with-lifelong-benefits)).

**Convention.** Mem Fox's craft position is that "Rhythm needs to be in the marrow of your bones if you're thinking of writing a picture book. I don't mean rhymes, necessarily", and that a text must be read aloud again and again until the rhythm is perfect ([Mem Fox](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/writing-picture-books)). Donaldson says the same from the other direction.

**Inference.** A rhyme or refrain is a built-in completion prompt: a line that stops before a predictable word invites the child to supply it, which is the behaviour dialogic reading trains adults to elicit. Prompts can therefore be designed into a book or app, a question on the page, a pause before a rhyme, a "have you ever?" at the end, so that the adult needs no training. A book built for the tenth reading has a refrain to join, picture detail to discover later, and a few stretch words that become learnable once the plot is known. Extending the re-reading finding from three-year-olds to babies is itself an inference.

### On screens, features that carry the story help and extras hurt

**Guidance.** The Australian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines recommend **no sedentary screen time under two and no more than one hour a day from two to five**, with reading and storytelling with a caregiver encouraged when a child is sedentary ([Department of Health](https://www.health.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/2021/05/24-hour-movement-guidelines-birth-to-5-years-fact-sheet.pdf)). The WHO sets the no-screen line at under one and the same one-hour limit to age four ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more)). The AAP's toolkit on e-books says parents and children are chattier and snuggle more with print, that "bells and whistles" distract or take over the adult's role, that children learn best from a simple, streamlined design, and that e-books are best avoided at bedtime or before naps ([AAP toolkit](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/electronic-books-and-audio-books-for-children-0-5-years-of-age/)). Apple's Kids Category rules forbid links out, purchasing opportunities and other distractions unless behind a parental gate, say such apps should not include third-party analytics or advertising, and require a privacy policy ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)).

**Research.** A 2015 meta-analysis found that multimedia features (animated pictures, music, sound effects) benefited story comprehension and vocabulary, while interactive features (hotspots, games, dictionaries) distracted, most of all for children from less stimulating homes ([Takacs, Swart & Bus 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647204)). A 2021 meta-analysis of **39 studies and 1,812 children** found that a plain digitised book produced lower comprehension than paper, that an adult mediating print beat digital enhancements used by a child alone, and that digital books with story-congruent enhancements outperformed paper ([Furenes, Kucirkova & Bus 2021](https://oro.open.ac.uk/75652)). In a study of 37 parent-toddler pairs, print produced more dialogic talk, and tablets produced more talk about the device, even with basic e-books; the sample was small and the readings lasted five minutes ([Reach Out and Read](https://reachoutandread.org/article/differences-in-parent-toddler-interactions-with-electronic-versus-print-books/)). Eye-tracking in pre-kindergarten found about 82% of fixations on pictures and 16% on text during narrated e-books, with little word learning from highlighting ([Arslan-Ari & Ari 2021](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15391523.2021.1938763)). Tapping and swiping alone do not count as active learning in the Hirsh-Pasek framework ([Association for Psychological Science](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/educational-apps.html)). Most of these studies cover ages three to six; evidence for under-twos is thin, and no study of digital lift-the-flap mechanics or of phone against tablet was found.

**Convention.** Sesame Workshop's practitioner guidance says tapping is the most intuitive gesture for preschoolers, that they hold tablets in landscape, that hotspots should stay away from the bottom edge where wrists rest, and that e-books should offer modes with and without narration ([Cooney Center](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/publication/best-practices-designing-touch-tablet-experiences-for-preschoolers/)). Nielsen Norman Group recommends touch targets of at least 2 cm square for young children and tap-then-tap as an alternative to drag-and-drop ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/children-ux-physical-development/)). Nosy Crow's stated principle is "reading experiences and not just games", with separate read-and-play and read-by-myself modes ([Nosy Crow](https://nosycrow.com/blog/appy-days)).

**Inference.** The working rule is that an enhancement is safe if removing it would make the story harder to follow, and suspect if the story reads the same without it. A digital flap is an interactive feature, so it falls on the risky side of that line unless the flap is the story beat: the reveal answers the question the text has just asked. One flap per spread, on the narrative path, opened by a tap, with nothing else tappable and no dead end if it is never touched, is the configuration most consistent with the evidence. The tablet study implies that the biggest win is invisible mechanics: one gesture, no settings inside the story, no accidental exits. Read-together mode, with narration off and the adult as the voice, should be the default for under-threes, and the story must work fully with the sound off; no source settled what the default sound state should be. On a phone, a recomposed single scene with one or two short sentences is more workable than a shrunken spread; that figure is a board-book convention, not a tested number. The app is on the most defensible ground when it is positioned for two to five, with the adult reading to any younger sibling, kept short with natural stopping points, and not pitched at bedtime. A low-stimulation bedtime mode is consistent with the cautions but no body endorses bedtime screens at all.

### What this means for the platypus series

Everything in this section and the two that follow is inference: recommendations drawn from the evidence above for a toddler platypus, her baby sibling, parents, grandparents, and echidna, koala, wombat and quokka friends.

#### Match the engine to the age, and reserve flaps for reveals

For the youngest readers the series should use the smallest story units. Under two, with the adult holding the device and reading, that means a quiet Miffy-like sequence of single images or a peek-a-boo reveal, in very few words. From one to three the natural shapes are the routine walked through step by step, the search with a reveal, and the chant that ends on a question. From two to four, count-and-calendar and offer-and-reject patterns add a visible break in the pattern. From three to five the series can carry real plots: three encounters and a return, repeated failure with a reliable helper, and a game with an emotional snag. The busy explorable scene spans the whole range because a child can use whichever layer suits them: naming, tiny incidents, or a hidden thing to find.

Lift-the-flap stories fit the one-to-three band, where the flap turns the adult's question into the child's action, but the flap must be the answer to the text and not decoration, and vocabulary-teaching pages should stay plain. Because a toddler lead and a baby cannot carry dialogue, the text voice should narrate around them, in present tense where the child acts on the page, and it should be written for an adult's mouth and tested aloud until the rhythm holds. Rhyme is optional and unforgiving; a refrain and a steady beat are the safer tools. One fixed final spread across the series, a return to the burrow, a parent, or sleep, gives the ritual ending the television series all have. The Bluey team's weekly family screenings suggest a cheap test: read drafts aloud to real children and watch where attention drops.

#### Profiles built from exact ages, wants and a line to say

Industry guidance on series bibles names four parts, the world, the characters, the stories and the format, with character entries covering background, goals, emotional tone, look and relationships ([AnimationXpress](https://www.animationxpress.com/latest-news/guest-column-htwfa-31-the-four-vital-elements-of-an-animated-series-bible/)). Writer Tim Bain adds that preschool writing means focusing on a character's innermost emotions and that adults "definitely take a back seat" ([Skwigly](https://www.skwigly.co.uk/tim-bain-writing-childrens-kids-animation/)). No published preschool bible was found, and the phrase "story engine" appeared in no source. The profile template the researcher assembled from these sources and from how Bluey's cast is differentiated runs as follows: name; exact age; one-line essence; look, meaning colour, shape and signature object; want; fear or soft spot; age-true flaw; recurring behaviour; how they speak, including vocabulary size, catchphrase or sound; home and family circumstance; relationship to each other regular; how they behave under pressure; and what they never do. The fear line and the never-does line are common practice in the researcher's experience but were not found stated in any source.

The series bible should add four short pages around those profiles. A one-sentence engine that generates stories, with ten or more springboards to prove it runs. World rules: whose point of view, whether adults can solve the problem (normally not), whether imagination is shown as real, what never appears, and how every story ends. A values statement that says what the series is for and what it deliberately does not teach. And a format page giving length, age band and the role of sound.

For the toddler lead, the profile should list a handful of real words used consistently, big fast-changing feelings, a strong will, and action carried by the pictures. For the baby, it should list what she can do at this exact age (grab, crawl, point, drop, laugh), one signature sound, one thing she always wants, and who interprets her; she works best as a source of interruptions that redirect the toddler's game and as someone the toddler has feelings about. Letting the baby develop across the series, as Bluey's youngest cousin moves from barking to sentences, rewards repeat readers. Parents earn their place by joining the game on the child's terms and by having one honest small feeling per story, tired or distracted, without solving the problem. Grandparents work as the ones the child teaches, with more time, looser rules and a different house.

#### Five levers keep nine characters apart

The classics separate large casts with the same few levers, drawn by the researcher from the work and not from any maker's stated rules: a name that does work through its sound; a silhouette recognisable in solid black; one trait or want; a repeated line or sound that is how a non-reader "says" the character; and a fixed place in the world. Bluey adds exact, spaced ages, so that each child wants and can do different things in the same game, and gives each friend a home circumstance that generates stories without becoming a "topic". Its flaws are age-true and not moral failings.

Applied here, the four friends are different species, which already supplies silhouette and colour, as breed does in Bluey and species does in Spot, Maisy and Peppa. Each friend should then carry one prop or colour never shared with another, a name with a distinct sound and syllable count, a like or a skill in place of a fault, a line or sound a child can say, and a home the child can find on a map of the world. The family is the harder problem, because the platypuses all share one silhouette: size, exact age, one signature object each and distinct speech patterns have to do the work that species does for the friends. Roles and competences should be spread deliberately across genders from the start, which is the lesson of both the Mr. Men research and the Scarry revisions. New characters are best introduced a few at a time, with the whole cast together reserved for busy-scene formats, and one small recurring thing hidden in every story gives a pre-reader a task they can do better than the adult.

### Koala and wombat are crowded; structure is free to borrow

**What the sources document.** Australian picture books for the very young lean on native animals, a chantable patterned text, dry humour and specific real places. Where is the Green Sheep? is a patterned chant that ends on a question ([Mem Fox](https://memfox.com/books/where-is-the-green-sheep/)); Wombat Stew turns on a repeated chant and a dingo outwitted by a platypus, emu, lizard, koala and echidna ([Better Beginnings](https://www.better-beginnings.com.au/book-finder/wombat-stew)); Diary of a Wombat gets its humour from a wombat's terse diary voice ([Wikipedia: Diary of a Wombat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diary_of_a_Wombat)). Existing characters crowd two of the five species. Koalas have Blinky Bill, in print since 1933, and Koala Lou ([Wikipedia: Blinky Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blinky_Bill)). Wombats have the Muddle-Headed Wombat and Mothball of Diary of a Wombat ([Wikipedia: List of fictional marsupials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fictional_marsupials)). Platypus, echidna and quokka have many picture books, among them Shy the Platypus, The Echidna Near My Place and Clancy the Quokka, but in the sources found no single dominant Australian picture-book character, and no quokka appears in Wikipedia's list of fictional marsupials. That search was incomplete, because the list of fictional monotremes could not be opened.

On First Nations content, the settled industry position is that stories, languages, designs and knowledge are Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property held by communities; the Australian Society of Authors says copyright law protects it inadequately and that authors are expected to follow the protocols ([Australian Society of Authors](https://www.asauthors.org.au/news/what-you-need-to-know-about-indigenous-cultural-and-intellectual-property/)). AIATSIS's test is prior and informed consent and benefit to the community in ways it values ([AIATSIS](https://aiatsis.gov.au/aboriginal-studies-press/getting-published/ethical-publishing-guidelines)). No guidance specific to apps or to animal-only stories was found.

**Inference.** Structure is free to use and expression is not. The transferable principles are one engine per book, a fixed reusable container, one action per opening, five to eight beats and a break, a thin lead with a secure home, flat colour and empty backgrounds, and text short enough to memorise. What is distinctive and should not be imitated is Bruna's face construction and colour set, Hill's dog-behind-furniture search, Carle's eaten holes through food and painted-tissue look, Campbell's crates from the zoo, a hidden creature that reads as Scarry's gold bug, and a Hargreaves-style name-equals-trait cast. Within species, the stock traits to avoid or knowingly play against are the cheeky koala in red dungarees or the sleepy cuddly one; the stubborn, hungry, deadpan wombat or the lovably muddled one; the smiling snack-thief quokka; the shy, spiky, ant-loving echidna; and the shy odd-one-out platypus in a female life-cycle story. Names to steer clear of include Blinky, Nutsy, Mothball, Hush, Grandma Poss, Koala Lou, Clancy for a quokka, Shy and Poppy for a platypus, Eric and Spikey for an echidna, and Walter for a wombat. The wombat and koala friends need the most deliberate design and voice. Bruna's own advice on imitation applies: "Try to make something that you think of yourself" ([Wikipedia: Miffy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy)). None of this is legal advice, and a trademark search on character names is a separate step.

An original animal cast in an invented but recognisably Australian setting, with accurate natural history, sits outside the cultural-property concerns and is the low-risk path. The things to avoid are inventing Dreamtime-style origin stories for the animals, imitating First Nations art styles in illustration or interface, coining names from First Nations language lists, and presenting the bush as empty or discovered. If a First Nations character, language, story, artwork or culturally significant real place enters a story, that is the point to seek a genuine partnership with the relevant community, with consent, attribution and benefit-sharing.

### Twelve story shapes to outline first

This starting set is inference throughout. Each shape names the classic that demonstrates the structure and states what makes the new version different, so that the structure is borrowed and the expression is not. The premises are illustrations to be replaced, not proposals.

| Shape | Engine in one line | Age and format | Demonstrated by | What keeps it original |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quiet sequence | An outing or bedtime as a run of single calm images, ending in sleep | 0 to 2, adult-read; 8 to 12 screens, a line or two each | Miffy | Own drawing style and palette; creek and burrow setting; no quatrain imitation |
| Routine walk-through | One first experience, step by step | 1 to 3; picture story, optional single reveal | Maisy, Spot's firsts | The specifics of a platypus household: bath in the creek, a visit to the grandparents |
| Search and reveal | A question per spread; the flap answers it; the last flap finds the thing | 1 to 3; lift-the-flap, one flap per spread | Where's Spot?, Where is the Green Sheep? | Outdoor hiding places from the habitat; each wrong reveal gives a clue instead of a "no" |
| Chant with a question | Paired lines build to a repeated question the page-turn answers | 1.5 to 3; picture story or flap | Where is the Green Sheep? | A refrain tied to the toddler's own want |
| Count over a day | A number pattern laid over a time pattern, one break, a warm end | 2 to 4; picture story | The Very Hungry Caterpillar | No eating-through device and no holes; collecting or building instead of eating |

A hard subject, such as the baby's arrival, a goodbye or a hospital visit, needs no shape of its own. The lesson from Bruna is to tell it in the series' normal format with one fact per page and a named feeling.

### Conclusion

The classics and the research point in the same direction from different starting points. Bruna, Hill and Carle reduced each book to one idea and one action because they were poster designers; the meta-analyses on digital books arrive at the same rule by measurement, that anything on the page which is not the story costs comprehension. For an app this turns an apparent handicap into a design brief. The screen's risk is extra features and device talk, and the classics' strength is having no extras at all, so a story app that behaves like a Bruna or Hill book, with one gesture, one reveal and an adult's voice, is working with the evidence and not against it.

The thinnest part of the evidence is exactly where this project sits: under-twos on screens, digital flaps, phones on laps, and how to write a toddler and a baby who barely speak. Those questions will not be answered by further reading. They will be answered by outlining the twelve shapes, drafting two or three, and reading them aloud to real children with the sound off, watching where a finger goes and where attention drops. Combined with a check of the unverified leads against physical copies of the books and a name search for the cast, that is the next piece of research worth doing.

### 1. What a child can attend to, understand and enjoy at each age band

#### Takeaway
Guidance bodies agree on a clear progression: looking and listening (0-6 months), handling and peek-a-boo (7-12 months), naming and predicting (1-3 years), then following and joining in with real stories (3-5 years). The only research-grade developmental detail I found is on humour; attention-span and fear figures were not sourced.

#### Cited Findings

**0-12 months**
- [Guidance] 0-6 months: babies recognise familiar voices and explore books by touch. Suitable content is rhyming text or songs, lullabies for bedtime, large baby faces, and animals an adult can point to and make sounds for. — [AAP, Choosing Books for Children and Families](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/choosing-books-for-children-and-families/)
- [Guidance] Newborn eyesight is not fully developed; babies see best at about 12 inches. Pages should hold one or two objects, plenty of empty space and very few words. — [Nemours Reading BrightStart](https://www.nemours.org/reading-brightstart/articles-for-parents/how-to-select-books-for-different-ages.html)
- [Guidance] (snippet) For newborns choose visually simple books: one or two things per page, a simple background and "blocky" design rather than fine detail. Author is Prof. Anna Franklin, Sussex Baby Lab. Only the first of five tips was retrieved. — [BookTrust](https://booktrust.org.uk/news-and-features/features/2023/june/5-top-tips-for-choosing-baby-books)
- [Guidance] 7-12 months: babies handle books on their own, explore textures and build fine motor skills. They like photos of babies and familiar objects, bright colours with simple images, and flaps for peek-a-boo. — [AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/choosing-books-for-children-and-families/)
- [Guidance] Older infants begin to learn that pictures have meaning and enjoy rhythmic, rhyming text. A single word paired with a picture, simple sentences or nursery rhymes suit them. — [Nemours](https://www.nemours.org/reading-brightstart/articles-for-parents/how-to-select-books-for-different-ages.html)

**1-2 years**
- [Guidance] Toddlers name objects, predict what comes next in repetitive text, and begin to name and handle emotions. Content: colourful pictures of real-life objects to find and name, simple text with repetition or rhyme, everyday topics such as bedtime and feelings, and silly or funny books. — [AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/choosing-books-for-children-and-families/)
- [Guidance] Toddlers move constantly and have a short attention span but still benefit from daily reading. If a story is too long, talk about the pictures instead; favourite characters do not guarantee attention. — [Nemours](https://www.nemours.org/reading-brightstart/articles-for-parents/how-to-select-books-for-different-ages.html)
- [Guidance] (snippet) 12-24 months: choose familiar characters and action pictures, because the child can now enjoy pictures with more detail. Attributed to Zero to Three via reprints; the original Zero to Three page was not found. — [Idaho Commission for Libraries tip sheet](https://libraries.idaho.gov/files/Tips%20for%20Choosing%20Age%20Appropriate%20Books.pdf)

**2-3 years**
- [Guidance] Around 30 months children can often turn paper pages and enjoy picture books, not only board books. — [AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/choosing-books-for-children-and-families/)
- [Guidance] (snippet) 24-36 months: predictable books with repeated text, rhyming words and pictures that correspond to the text. — [Idaho Commission for Libraries tip sheet](https://libraries.idaho.gov/files/Tips%20for%20Choosing%20Age%20Appropriate%20Books.pdf)
- [Guidance] Toddlers can memorise and "read" or sing along with repetitive text; they like playful, funny-sounding words. — [Nemours](https://www.nemours.org/reading-brightstart/articles-for-parents/how-to-select-books-for-different-ages.html)

**3-5 years**
- [Guidance] Preschoolers follow more complex stories, remember repeated phrases, and enjoy predicting what happens next, filling in blanks and guessing the next word. The story should move quickly enough to finish in one sitting, with a basic plot. — [Nemours](https://www.nemours.org/reading-brightstart/articles-for-parents/how-to-select-books-for-different-ages.html)
- [Guidance] Preschoolers suit longer stories; text they can start to memorise and fill in; topics tied to their interests (animals, transport, science); familiar themes (school, friendship, emotions); unfamiliar topics that build empathy; counting, alphabet and rhyming books. — [AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/choosing-books-for-children-and-families/)

**Humour**
- [Research] (snippet) Hoicka & Akhtar (2012) filmed 47 parent-child pairs. Two-year-olds relied mainly on object-based humour (using objects the wrong way). Three-year-olds made more conceptual humour and trended towards label-based humour (deliberately calling things the wrong name). Two-year-olds copied a parent's joke as often as they invented one; three-year-olds mostly invented their own. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/jokes-toddlers-make)
- [Research] (snippet) The Early Humor Survey (parent report, 671 children aged 0-47 months) identified 21 types of humour. Mislabelling emerges around age two and most three-year-olds can do it. — [University of Bristol news, 2021](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2021/november/humour-study.html)

#### Inferences
- Humour in books should track the humour children produce: physical and object-based silliness for under-threes (a hat on a foot), wrong-name and wrong-category jokes from about two and a half to three onwards.
- "Finish in one sitting" plus "talk about the pictures if it is too long" implies art should carry the story on its own for under-threes, so a spread works even when the text is skipped.
- The 30-month paper-page marker is a practical dividing line between board-book and paper picture-book formats.

### 2. Formats, page counts, trim sizes and word counts

#### Takeaway
Publishing figures are conventions, not research, and sources disagree at the edges: roughly under 50 words for baby board books, under 200 for older board books, and about 500 (ceiling 600-800) for a 32-page picture book. The 32-page standard comes from offset printing in 8- and 16-page signatures.

#### Cited Findings

**Formats by age**
- [Guidance] 0-6 months: high-contrast books for tummy time, soft cloth books, books with textures. — [AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/choosing-books-for-children-and-families/)
- [Guidance] Newborns: small books a caregiver can hold and turn one-handed while holding the baby; black-and-white or bright high-contrast images; soft vinyl or cloth that survives bath, chewing and throwing. — [Nemours](https://www.nemours.org/reading-brightstart/articles-for-parents/how-to-select-books-for-different-ages.html)
- [Guidance] (snippet) Black-and-white or bold bright books stand out against an otherwise blurry world for young babies. — [Scottish Book Trust](https://scottishbooktrust.com/articles/top-tips-choosing-books-for-wee-ones)
- [Guidance] 7-12 months: sturdy board or cloth books in a small size the baby can handle alone; textured pages; flaps. — [AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/choosing-books-for-children-and-families/)
- [Guidance] Older infants: board books with rounded edges and thick pages; peek-a-boo, pop-up and lift-the-flap; textures, scents and mirrors. — [Nemours](https://www.nemours.org/reading-brightstart/articles-for-parents/how-to-select-books-for-different-ages.html)
- [Guidance] Toddlers: small sturdy board books with few or no words; wordless books; lift-the-flap and touch-and-feel; concept books (animals, feelings, colours, numbers, shapes, letters). — [Nemours](https://www.nemours.org/reading-brightstart/articles-for-parents/how-to-select-books-for-different-ages.html)
- [Guidance] (snippet) 12-24 months: books with movable parts such as sliders, wheels and die-cut finger trails build hand-eye coordination. The originating source for this line was not identified. — [Idaho Commission for Libraries tip sheet](https://libraries.idaho.gov/files/Tips%20for%20Choosing%20Age%20Appropriate%20Books.pdf)
- [Research] (snippet) Counter-evidence on flaps: Shinskey tested 32 two-year-olds with a commercial "first words" book, with flaps or with the flaps sealed. Only the no-flap group learned the new word (carambola) above chance. The authors' explanation is that the flaps pull attention from the content. Press coverage gives 31 children; the paper says 32. — [Royal Holloway research portal](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/lift-the-flap-features-in-first-words-picture-books-impede-word-l)
- [Research] (snippet, secondhand via a conference slide deck) Drawings and cartoons, compared with photographs, reduced word learning in 15- and 18-month-olds (Ganea et al., 2008). Pop-up style manipulative features reduced word learning in 20-month-olds and 30-36-month-olds (Tare et al., 2010). Neither paper was read. — [Royal Holloway event slides](https://su.royalholloway.ac.uk/media/20868/amber%20muhinyi%20research%20and%20practice%20event%2022.06.22.pdf.pdf)

**Word counts and page counts (all conventions)**
- [Convention] Miranda Paul (author and Highlights Foundation faculty) gives her "personal references", not official standards: ages 0-3, under 50 words, board book of 8-24 pages; ages 2-5, under 200 words, board book of 24-32 pages; ages 4-8, at or under 500 words, 32-page picture book. Counts exclude back matter. Nonfiction can run to roughly double. Her own picture books range from 147 to 1,400 words. — [Boyds Mills / Highlights Foundation](https://boydsmills.org/2019/04/08/picture-book-word-count-and-other-rules-meant-to-be-broken/)
- [Convention] (snippet) Other guides for picture books: 400-800 words with 500 as the safe target; 300-800 with about 500 as the aim; "usually 500-600". Editor Mary Kole is cited as saying 600 maximum. These are blogs and self-publishing sites, not publisher guidelines. — [Writers in the Storm](https://writersinthestormblog.com/2025/02/picture-book-word-counts-minimalism-to-modern-stories/); [Mary Kole](https://marykole.squarespace.com/how-to-write-childrens-books)
- [Convention] (snippet) Board books: about 100 words or fewer in most guides; page counts quoted variously as 10-12, 12-24, and "16 is standard". — [WriteForKids](https://writeforkids.online/childrens-book-word-counts-by-age-group/)
- [Convention] (snippet) Printer specification: board books run from 6 to 20 leaves, which is 12 to 40 printed sides, and the count must be even. Sizes generally run from 5 x 5 inches to 10 x 10 inches. — [PrintNinja](https://printninja.com/printing-resource-center/book-game-industry-standards/childrens-books/board-books)

**Why 32 pages**
- [Convention] Books are printed in signatures: one large press sheet printed both sides and folded to page size, each fold doubling the page count. 16-page and 8-page signatures are the most common. A 32-page book is two 16s or four 8s; a 40-page book is five 8s. Filling whole signatures keeps cost down. Picture-book pages are large, so fewer fit on a sheet than for a novel. This applies to offset printing only; digital printing differs. Author is Danielle Carnito, Trade Art Director, Lerner. — [Lerner Books blog](https://lernerbooks.blog/2018/01/picture-book-page-counts-demystified.html)
- [Convention] Odd counts such as 36 exist (two 16s plus a 4-page signature) but small signatures are not always recommended. Endsheets are not included in the page count. — [Lerner Books blog](https://lernerbooks.blog/2018/01/picture-book-page-counts-demystified.html)
- [Convention] (snippet) 24, 40 and 48 pages are the common alternatives, and publishers generally pay more to depart from 32. — [Debbie Ridpath Ohi, Picture Books 101](https://debbieohi.substack.com/p/picture-books-101-part-3-understanding)

#### Inferences
- Counting conventions differ: printers count board-book printed sides (12 sides = 6 leaves), while writers' guides say "pages" loosely. A "12-page board book" is the printer's minimum, about 5 spreads plus covers.
- A 32-page picture book does not give 32 pages of story. Title, copyright and (if self-ended) endpapers take pages; the commonly quoted working figure is 12-14 story spreads, but I did not source that number here.
- The flap and realism findings suggest keeping pure "first words" naming books plain and photographic or realistic, and reserving flaps for play books (peek-a-boo, surprise) where the reveal is the content.
- In-app stories are not bound by signatures, so page count there can follow the child's attention instead of the press sheet.

#### Gaps
- No publisher's own submission guideline was found stating board-book or picture-book word counts. SCBWI and Reedsy were not reached. Every word-count figure above is an author's or blogger's rule of thumb.
- No sourced picture-book trim sizes (for example 8 x 10 or 10 x 10 inches in the US, or UK and Australian equivalents). The only size figure is the printer's 5-10 inch square range for board books.
- No UK- or Australia-specific publishing conventions found. UK picture books are widely said to follow the same 32-page, roughly 12-spread form, but I did not verify this.
- No figures for cloth, bath or touch-and-feel book page counts.
- The Shinskey, Ganea and Tare findings are small lab studies read at snippet level; sample sizes and exact results need checking against the papers.

### 3. Language devices and what each does

#### Takeaway
Guidance bodies consistently recommend rhyme, rhythm, repetition and predictable text because they let a child anticipate and join in; research adds that book language is richer than everyday speech to children. I found no study isolating the effect of individual devices such as refrain, cumulative structure or onomatopoeia.

#### Cited Findings
- [Research] (snippet) Montag, Jones & Smith (2015, Psychological Science) compared a corpus of 100 picture books with a matched sample of child-directed speech. The books contained more unique word types at any sample size. Press coverage put the gap at roughly 70% more, a figure the author said depends on method. — [UEA repository record](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/69099/)
- [Research] (snippet) Dawson et al. (2021) found children's book language is lexically denser, more diverse and has a larger share of rarer word types than child-directed speech. They cite Massaro (2015): picture books hold about three times the rare word types of child-directed speech. The Massaro figure is secondhand. — [Nuffield Foundation PDF](https://www.nuffieldfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Features-of-lexical-richness-in-childrens-books-Comparisons-with-child-directed-speech.pdf)
- [Guidance] (snippet) Babies respond well to books with rhyme, rhythm and repetition, and repetition helps children learn. — [Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/babies/play-learning/literacy-reading-stories/reading-from-birth)
- [Guidance] Repetition and rhyme in simple text let 1-2 year olds predict what comes next. Preschoolers benefit from text they can memorise and fill in. — [AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/choosing-books-for-children-and-families/)
- [Guidance] Naming and labelling: a single word with a picture for older infants; pictures of real-life objects to find and name for toddlers; animals to point at and make sounds for with young babies. — [Nemours](https://www.nemours.org/reading-brightstart/articles-for-parents/how-to-select-books-for-different-ages.html); [AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/choosing-books-for-children-and-families/)
- [Guidance] Songs, poetry and repetitive text are easy for toddlers to memorise so they can read or sing along. Predictable text, rhymes and funny-sounding words suit toddlers. Preschoolers enjoy filling in the blank and guessing the next word. — [Nemours](https://www.nemours.org/reading-brightstart/articles-for-parents/how-to-select-books-for-different-ages.html)
- [Research] Fill-in-the-blank is formalised in dialogic reading as the Completion prompt, one of the five CROWD prompt types. See section 4. — [Victoria Department of Education](https://www.education.vic.gov.au/school/teachers/teachingresources/discipline/english/literacy/readingviewing/Pages/dialogic-reading.aspx)

#### Inferences
- Rhyme and refrain work as built-in completion prompts: a line that stops just before a predictable rhyming word invites the child to supply it, which is the behaviour dialogic reading trains adults to elicit.
- Because books supply words that everyday talk does not, a text for under-fives can include a few rich or unusual words provided the pictures and repetition support them.
- Question-and-answer text ("Where is...? There!") scripts the conversation for a parent who would not otherwise ask questions.

#### Gaps
- No research found on the specific effect of rhyme on phonological awareness in under-fives, though this literature exists (Bryant and Bradley's nursery rhyme work is the usual citation; not verified here).
- Nothing sourced on cumulative structure, onomatopoeia or question-and-answer formats individually.
- Raising Children Network's page returned 403; the line above is from a search summary only.
- No CLPE material was found on patterned language or core books.

### 4. Shared reading evidence: dialogic reading, effects on vocabulary, re-reading

#### Takeaway
Shared reading helps language, but the best recent meta-analysis puts the average effect small (about 0.19) and sensitive to study design, while dialogic reading shows a moderate effect on expressive vocabulary that is stronger in younger children. Re-reading the same book beats reading several different ones for retaining new words.

#### Cited Findings

**Dialogic reading**
- [Research] (snippet) Dialogic reading was developed by Grover Whitehurst and colleagues at Stony Brook, dating to Whitehurst et al. (1988). The child is made an active participant and the adult becomes the listener and questioner. — [EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/language-and-linguistics/dialogic-reading)
- [Research] (snippet) PEER is the adult's sequence: Prompt the child to say something, Evaluate the response, Expand it, Repeat the prompt so the child uses the expansion. — [Victoria Department of Education](https://www.education.vic.gov.au/school/teachers/teachingresources/discipline/english/literacy/readingviewing/Pages/dialogic-reading.aspx)
- [Research] (snippet) CROWD names five prompt types: Completion, Recall, Open-ended, Wh- questions, and Distancing (linking the book to the child's own experience). Unlike PEER, CROWD is a set, not a fixed order. — [Victoria Department of Education](https://www.education.vic.gov.au/school/teachers/teachingresources/discipline/english/literacy/readingviewing/Pages/dialogic-reading.aspx)
- [Research] (snippet) The US What Works Clearinghouse intervention report on Dialogic Reading drew on four randomised controlled trials in centre-based settings. — [WWC report copy](https://corpora.tika.apache.org/base/docs/govdocs1/292/292392.pdf)
- [Research] (snippet) Mol, Bus, de Jong & Smeets (2008) pooled 16 studies comparing dialogic with ordinary parent-child reading. For expressive vocabulary (9 studies, 322 children) Cohen's d was 0.59, a moderate effect. The effect reduced substantially for older children. — [Leiden University repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2899524/view)

**Shared reading overall**
- [Research] (snippet) Noble et al. (2019, Educational Research Review) found an overall effect of shared book reading on language of about 0.19, smaller than earlier meta-analyses. It was moderated by the type of control group and negligible in some designs. Dialogic technique did not significantly moderate outcomes in this analysis. — [Noble et al. 2019 PDF](https://hira.hope.ac.uk/3756/1/Noble%20et%20al.%20%282019%29.pdf)
- [Research] (snippet) Flack, Field & Horst (2018) meta-analysed word learning from shared storybook reading across 38 studies and 2,455 children. — [Flack, Field & Horst](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-effects-of-shared-storybook-reading-on-word-learning-a-2vvwqjdaa8.pdf)
- [Research] (snippet) Reach Out and Read hosts a 2022 systematic review of the dialogic reading literature (Pillinger). Contents not read. — [Reach Out and Read](https://reachoutandread.org/pillinger_2022_a-story-so-far-a-systematic-review-of-the-dialogic-reading-literature)

**Re-reading**
- [Research] (snippet) Horst, Parsons & Bryan (2011): three-year-olds heard either the same story three times or three different stories containing the same two novel words. All did well immediately afterwards, but only the same-story group retained the words a week later. — [Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00017/pdf)
- [Research] (snippet) Horst's 2013 review proposes that contextual repetition lowers the attentional demands of word learning: once the story is familiar, the child can attend to the new words. — [Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00149/pdf)
- [Guidance] Young children often want the same story many times; choose books adult and child both enjoy. — [Nemours](https://www.nemours.org/reading-brightstart/articles-for-parents/how-to-select-books-for-different-ages.html)

#### Inferences
- The conflict between Mol 2008 (dialogic adds a moderate benefit) and Noble 2019 (dialogic is not a significant moderator; overall effect small) should be stated plainly. A fair reading: interaction helps most with two- and three-year-olds and with expressive vocabulary, and claims of large effects are not supported by the stricter trials.
- A book suited to re-reading rewards the tenth read: a refrain to join in with, picture detail to discover later, and a few stretch words that become learnable once the plot is known.
- Dialogic prompts can be designed into a book or app (a question on the page, a pause before a rhyme, a "have you ever...?" at the end) so the adult does not need training.
- Horst's studies used purpose-made books with small samples of three-year-olds; extending the finding to babies is an inference.

#### Gaps
- Reading Rockets' Whitehurst article (the standard source for PEER and CROWD examples) returned 403. From memory, not verified this session: Whitehurst claims children read to dialogically can jump ahead by several months in language after a few weeks, and recommends dialogic reading mainly from age two onwards.
- Serve-and-return (Harvard Center on the Developing Child) was not researched. No source collected.
- Noble 2019 and Horst 2011 full texts could not be opened; exact numbers (study counts, word-retention scores, sample sizes) are missing.
- The exact age comparison in Mol 2008 (2-3 versus 4-5 year olds) was not retrieved.
- No research found on why children ask for the same book beyond Horst's attentional account (for example comfort, mastery and predictability).

### 2. Story structure and the "engine" of each book

#### Takeaway
Each book runs on one simple, visible engine that a child can predict after two openings: Miffy on a fixed 12-beat everyday outing or event that always ends well; Spot on a search with a wrong answer behind every flap until the last; the Caterpillar on a count laid over the days of the week, broken by a comic excess and resolved by transformation.

#### Cited Findings
- Miffy: twelve story pages, each one picture and one four-line verse; plots are everyday experiences and "always end happily". — [Wikipedia: Miffy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy)
- Where's Spot?: the story "mostly involves Sally wandering the house looking for Spot". — [Wikipedia: Spot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Where%27s_Spot%3F)
- Hill built the book as a seek-and-find story from a flap mechanism his small son enjoyed. — [Publishers Weekly obituary](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/obituaries/article/62808-obituary-eric-hill.html)
- Publisher statement at Hill's death (as reported in obituary coverage; snippet): the flap "turned the reading of a Spot book into a glorious game of hide and seek". — [The Bookseller: Spot creator Eric Hill dies](https://thebookseller.com/news/spot-dog-creator-eric-hill-dies) (attribution to this URL not confirmed by opening the page)
- The Very Hungry Caterpillar, beat by beat: egg on a leaf; hatches on Sunday morning; Monday one apple, Tuesday two pears, Wednesday three plums, Thursday four strawberries, Friday five oranges; Saturday a feast of assorted party and junk foods and a stomach ache; Sunday one green leaf and he feels better; cocoon; after two weeks, a butterfly. — [Wikipedia: The Very Hungry Caterpillar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Very_Hungry_Caterpillar)
- The first version (A Week with Willi the Worm) had no real ending; when editor Ann Beneduce suggested a caterpillar, Carle immediately proposed the butterfly, and the ending fell into place. — [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind)
- Dear Zoo: a child writes to the zoo for a pet; the zoo sends a series of animals, each unsuitable for one stated reason (too big, too tall, too grumpy), until the right one arrives. — [1001 Stories](https://1001stories.co.uk/stories/dear-zoo)
- Maisy: "simple, structured storylines" drawn from toddler experience; the series itself began as a lift-the-flap concept. — [Wikipedia: Maisy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maisy_(book_series))

#### Inferences
- Shared skeleton: (1) one-opening set-up that states the want or the situation; (2) a run of near-identical beats, typically five to eight, each one opening long; (3) a break in the pattern (Saturday's feast; the last flap; the right pet); (4) a short, warm close that returns to safety (home, food, parent, sleep).
- Engines by type: Bruna = outing/occasion as a sequence of small observed moments (no antagonist, almost no plot); Hill = search with repeated wrong guesses; Carle = count plus calendar plus cumulative appetite, ending in metamorphosis; Campbell = repeated delivery and rejection with a single adjective per beat; Cousins = routine or first experience walked through step by step.
- In Spot and Dear Zoo the pattern beat and the page-turn are not the unit; the flap is. Each opening holds a question and its answer, so the book has two reveals per spread (turn, then lift).
- The Caterpillar is unusual in stacking four learning patterns (numbers 1-5, days, foods/colours, life cycle) on one narrative line without the text ever stopping to teach. This matches the Penguin article's list of what it teaches. — [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind)

#### Gaps

### 3. Language

#### Takeaway
All three use very short, declarative, pattern-bound text, but with different voices: Bruna a quiet third-person rhymed quatrain; Hill a string of spoken questions answered inside the flaps; Carle a past-tense storyteller's refrain that grows by one each day. No source gave sentence-level statistics.

#### Cited Findings
- Bruna: four lines per page, rhyming on lines 2 and 4 (an ABCB quatrain). — [Wikipedia: Miffy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy)
- Bruna typed his stories on a manual typewriter, separately from drawing. — [DesignCurial: Dick Bruna, the art of reduction](https://old.designcurial.com/news/miffy-creator-dick-bruna---the-art-of-reduction-270115-4497133/)
- The English name "Miffy" was coined by the first English translator, Olive Jones (snippet). — [LoveReading4Kids: Miffy](https://lovereading4kids.co.uk/book/10010/Miffy-by-Dick-Bruna.html)
- English text history: a 1995 translation by Patricia Crampton; the 2014 Simon & Schuster editions are re-translations by the poet Tony Mitton, credited on the copyright page as based on Crampton's (snippet from library/retailer records). — [CiNii record: Miffy and the new baby](https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BB23242575?l=en); [Simon & Schuster UK: Miffy](https://www.simonandschuster.co.uk/books/Miffy/Dick-Bruna/MIFFY/9781471120787)
- The stated purpose of the 2014 re-translation was "to appeal to a modern British audience", with "new translations of Bruna's original rhyming verse"; pictures unchanged. — [BBC report reposted on Beattie's Book Blog](https://beattiesbookblog.blogspot.com/2013/08/miffy-books-to-be-updated-for-modern.html)
- Bruna's stated aim was clear, direct storytelling for children rather than parents. — [Wikipedia: Miffy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy)
- Hill said he aimed to recognise that children have "far more intelligence and style" than adults credit. — [Wikipedia: Eric Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Hill)
- Carle: "rhythm and repetition" make the book easy for young children to follow. — [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind)
- Carle deliberately chose "cocoon" over the technically correct "chrysalis", for its sound and for a family saying about coming out of one's cocoon. — [eric-carle.com FAQ](https://eric-carle.com/faq/); [Wikipedia: The Very Hungry Caterpillar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Very_Hungry_Caterpillar)
- Dear Zoo: simple text with repeating phrases, "ideal for the pre-reader" (snippet, retailer copy). — [QBD listing: Dear Zoo](https://www.qbd.com.au/dear-zoo/rod-campbell/9781529027402)

#### Inferences
- Bruna's ABCB form is forgiving for translators: only one rhyme pair per page has to be found, which is why English versions can stay close to the Dutch sense. The existence of three successive English texts (Jones, Crampton, Mitton) shows the verse dates faster than the pictures.
- Who is addressed: Bruna narrates about Miffy in the third person, often with Miffy speaking a line; Hill's text is a mother's voice asking questions that the child reader answers by lifting; Campbell is first person ("I wrote to the zoo"), which lets the child be the narrator; Carle is a classic third-person past-tense tale opening with a scene-setting sentence.
- Tense: Bruna and Carle past; Hill and Cousins present (dialogue or running commentary). Present-tense, second-person-adjacent text suits books where the child acts on the page.
- Carle's refrain is cumulative in number but constant in syntax, with a repeated tail clause about still being hungry; the Saturday list is the one long sentence in the book and is the comic release.

#### Gaps
- No source opened describes the Dutch originals' metre or compares Dutch and English line by line. UNVERIFIED RECALL: Bruna's Dutch lines are short, roughly three-beat lines, and English versions sometimes pad or invert to reach the rhyme, which was a stated criticism of the older translations.
- No Tony Mitton statement on his approach was found.
- Sentence length, word frequency and exact refrains for all three: not sourced, and deliberately not reproduced here.
- UNVERIFIED RECALL: in Where's Spot? the answers are single words in hand-lettered speech bubbles under the flaps, and the main text is set in very large bold type, one line per opening.

### 4. Design and interaction

#### Takeaway
Each physical decision gives the child one job: Bruna's square with one picture facing one block of text gives a single thing to look at; Hill's flap gives a hand action that answers the text's question; Carle's holes and stepped pages give a finger-sized path through the count.

#### Cited Findings
Bruna
- Drawing on the right, four lines of text on the left, in every opening (snippet). — [miffy.com: his books](https://www.miffy.com/his-books)
- The square format was originally imposed by paper costs; Bruna found it suited simplicity, and that children could hold the book themselves. — [DesignCurial](https://old.designcurial.com/news/miffy-creator-dick-bruna---the-art-of-reduction-270115-4497133/)
- Palette: black line plus a fixed small set of flat colours. Wikipedia lists nine including black and white (red, yellow, blue, green, orange, brown, grey). — [Wikipedia: Miffy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy)
- Colours are specific Bruna shades, inspired by De Stijl; influences named are Matisse (the Vence chapel cut-outs), Léger (heavy black outline), Picasso, Braque, the poster artists Savignac and Cassandre, and Dutch designers. — [DesignCurial](https://old.designcurial.com/news/miffy-creator-dick-bruna---the-art-of-reduction-270115-4497133/)
- Outline drawn by hand with brush and ink ("lines with a heartbeat"); the drawing is transferred to transparent film and cut coloured paper is laid beneath it to choose the colours. — [DesignCurial](https://old.designcurial.com/news/miffy-creator-dick-bruna---the-art-of-reduction-270115-4497133/); [Lateral Action: Miffy](https://lateralaction.com/articles/miffy/) (snippet)
- Miffy's face is "two dots and a cross"; Bruna worked through hundreds of drawings to get an expression. — [DesignCurial](https://old.designcurial.com/news/miffy-creator-dick-bruna---the-art-of-reduction-270115-4497133/)

Hill
- Origin of the flap: in the late 1970s Hill's two-year-old son Christopher was delighted by a lift-up flap on an advertising piece his father was designing; Hill built a story around it. Dates differ: 1976 (Wikipedia) versus 1978 (Publishers Weekly). — [Publishers Weekly obituary](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/obituaries/article/62808-obituary-eric-hill.html); [Wikipedia: Eric Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Hill)
- A handmade dummy reached the Frankfurt Book Fair through a friend, which led to publication. — [Publishers Weekly obituary](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/obituaries/article/62808-obituary-eric-hill.html)
- Hill is credited in obituaries with inventing the lift-the-flap picture book (snippet; Independent obituary headline as reported in search results). — [Kirkus: In memoriam](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/news-and-features/articles/memoriam-eric-hill-1927-2014)
- Spot's body spot and tail tip derive from aircraft markings Hill drew as a boy; he called the result a ready-made trademark. — [Wikipedia: Spot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Where%27s_Spot%3F)

Carle
- Idea came from punching holes in a stack of paper and thinking of a bookworm. — [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind)
- The die-cut holes are there so that a child can put a finger through the page. — [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind)
- No US printer could produce the die-cuts and irregular (different-width) pages economically; Beneduce found a printer in Japan through Hiroshi Imamura of Kaisei-sha. — [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind); [Wikipedia: The Very Hungry Caterpillar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Very_Hungry_Caterpillar)
- Technique: plain tissue paper painted with acrylics (brushes, fingers, stamps of carpet, sponge, burlap), stored by colour, cut and glued to white board; details added in coloured pencil. — [eric-carle.com FAQ](https://eric-carle.com/faq/); [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind)
- Critic Leonard Marcus: the simple surface hides layers, and the hole adds performance and make-believe. — [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind)

Comparisons
- Dear Zoo: the pleasure is "lifting the flaps to see which animals the zoo has sent". — [1001 Stories](https://1001stories.co.uk/stories/dear-zoo)
- Maisy: bold black outline with bright flat blocks of colour; Cousins starts with the black line and works in gouache. — [Carle Museum blog: Lucy Cousins painted our studio](https://carlemuseum.org/blogs/making-art/lucy-cousins-painted-our-studio) (snippet); [Wikipedia: Maisy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maisy_(book_series))

#### Inferences
- What each device does for the child. Flap: turns the adult's question into the child's action, gives object permanence play (peekaboo), and lets a pre-reader "read" the answer. Hole: gives a tactile trace of the character's path and a one-to-one count (one hole per fruit). Stepped pages: make the growing quantity visible from the first opening and give small hands an easy page edge. Bruna's fixed layout: removes all decisions about where to look, so the adult's voice and the single image arrive together.
- All three artists came from commercial graphic design (Bruna book jackets and posters, about 2,000 covers per DesignCurial; Hill advertising; Carle advertising). Poster logic (one idea, readable at a distance, flat colour, strong silhouette) is the common visual grammar.
- White or flat single-colour backgrounds are common to all five creators; nothing in the picture competes with the subject.

#### Gaps
- UNVERIFIED RECALL: Miffy is almost always drawn facing the reader, even when the body is in profile or seen walking away, and Bruna said this was so she always looks at the child. Widely reported in obituaries, but not confirmed in a source opened this session (Guardian obituary blocked).
- UNVERIFIED RECALL: the five fruit pages of the Caterpillar are narrow pages of increasing width (graduated), each with one die-cut hole per fruit, so that the week is visible as a staircase. The sources opened confirm "irregular binding" and holes but do not describe the graduated widths in detail.
- UNVERIFIED RECALL on Carle's other device-led books for the youngest: The Very Busy Spider (1984, raised web you can feel), The Very Quiet Cricket (1990, sound chip on last page), The Very Lonely Firefly (1995, lights), The Very Clumsy Click Beetle (1999, click sound); Brown Bear, Brown Bear (1967, text Bill Martin Jr, chain question-and-answer); 1, 2, 3 to the Zoo (1968, wordless counting); From Head to Toe (1997, action call-and-response). Only Brown Bear's connection was confirmed (Penguin UK). A retailer snippet did describe Carle's books as mixing toy and book, citing The Very Quiet Cricket, but the well-known Carle remark about a book you can play with and a toy you can read was not found in a primary source.
- Flap construction details (hinge side, shapes matching furniture, durability) for Spot and Dear Zoo: not sourced.

### 5. Character

#### Takeaway
The lead characters are deliberately thin: a child-proxy with almost no backstory, a secure family, and emotion carried by tiny graphic changes or by action rather than by description.

#### Cited Findings
- Miffy is a small rabbit with parents, Grandma and Grandpa, Auntie Alice, Uncle Bob and (later) a baby sibling; friends include Snuffy the dog (1969), Aggie and Winnie (1970), Poppy Pig and Grunty (1977), Melanie (1979), Farmer John (1982), Boris Bear (1986) and Barbara Bear (1989). — [Wikipedia: Miffy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy)
- Emotion is shown by minimal change: a single tear for sadness; tiny shifts of the eye dots and the mouth cross; a tilt of the head (snippets). — [The Conversation: Unpacking the magic of Miffy](https://theconversation.com/unpacking-the-magic-of-miffy-a-simply-drawn-bunny-shaped-friend-149725); [Lateral Action: Miffy](https://lateralaction.com/articles/miffy/)
- A curator's description: Miffy's simplicity is an illusion produced by many careful choices (snippet). — [It's Nice That: Dick Bruna feature](https://www.itsnicethat.com/features/dick-bruna-bruce-ingman-miffy-illustration-151020)
- Spot is a yellow puppy with mother Sally, father Sam and (from 1989) baby sister Susie; friends Helen the hippo, Steve the monkey and Tom the crocodile; grandparents appear in 1996. — [Wikipedia: Spot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Where%27s_Spot%3F)
- Hill on his characters: "They are my family, and Spot belongs to them all." — [Publishers Weekly obituary](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/obituaries/article/62808-obituary-eric-hill.html)
- The caterpillar has no name, no speech and no companions; Beneduce's reason for the switch from worm was that a green worm would not be a likeable protagonist. Carle called it a book of hope: a small, insignificant creature grows up and flies. — [Wikipedia: The Very Hungry Caterpillar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Very_Hungry_Caterpillar)
- Maisy began as a human child called Sally in a flap-book concept; editor Wendy Boase suggested an animal. Friends: Cyril (squirrel), Tallulah (chick), Charley (crocodile), Eddie (elephant). — [Wikipedia: Maisy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maisy_(book_series))

#### Inferences
- Degrees of personality on the page, low to high: caterpillar (pure appetite, a body the child can project onto) < Miffy (calm, curious, polite; feelings named briefly) < Spot (mildly mischievous, playful, the one who hides) < Maisy (busy, competent, independent, no parents shown).
- All are animals standing in for a child of unspecified age, sex-neutral in practice, which keeps them open to any child and easy to translate.
- Family is the safety net in Miffy and Spot (a parent starts and ends most stories); Maisy and the caterpillar manage alone, which gives a different, more autonomous fantasy.
- The friend casts in Spot and Maisy are different species in different colours, which functions as a recognisable set for small children and as a merchandising palette.

#### Gaps
- No interview source opened gives Bruna's, Hill's or Cousins's own description of the character's personality in detail (Horn Book "Five questions for Lucy Cousins" returned 403; Publishers Weekly Q&A not opened).
- UNVERIFIED RECALL: Maisy's friends and Maisy have no on-page parents; Spot's naughtiness is limited to hiding, getting muddy, opening presents early.

### 6. Themes and tone; how hard subjects are handled

#### Takeaway
The subject matter is the under-five's own calendar of firsts and outings, told without jeopardy. Bruna is the one who takes the fixed format into hospital, a new baby and a grandmother's death, and does it by keeping the same 12-beat calm rather than changing register.

#### Cited Findings
- Miffy plots cover everyday experiences such as going to hospital or school and always end happily. — [Wikipedia: Miffy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy)
- Dear Grandma Bunny deals with the death of Miffy's grandmother; it won the Dutch Silver Slate in 1997. Miffy in the Tent won a Silver Brush (1996); Boris Bear a Golden Brush (1990). — [Wikipedia: Miffy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy)
- Miffy and the New Baby and Miffy's Bicycle, Miffy at the Zoo, Miffy at the Gallery, Miffy at School, Miffy in the Snow are among the titles in the 2014 UK relaunch (snippets from publisher/retailer listings). — [Simon & Schuster UK: Miffy at School](https://www.simonandschuster.co.uk/books/Miffy-at-School/Dick-Bruna/MIFFY/9781471120831); [LoveReading4Kids: Miffy and the New Baby](https://lovereading4kids.co.uk/book/9966/Miffy-and-the-New-Baby-by-Dick-Bruna.html)
- Spot's titles are a list of firsts and occasions: first walk, birthday party, school, holiday, farm, first Easter, baby sister, a party, visiting grandparents. — [Wikipedia: Spot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Where%27s_Spot%3F)
- The Caterpillar's one "hard" moment is over-eating and a stomach ache, cured by a plain leaf; the book has been used by the American Academy of Pediatrics and CDC-linked healthy-eating campaigns, and read variously as religious allegory and as a comment on consumption. — [Wikipedia: The Very Hungry Caterpillar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Very_Hungry_Caterpillar)
- Its ending about growing up and moving on is cited as the source of its emotional pull. — [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind)

#### Inferences
- Tone is uniformly reassuring: no villains, no punishment, no lesson stated. Misbehaviour (Spot hiding, the caterpillar gorging) has a mild natural consequence and immediate repair.
- Hard subjects are handled by scale, not avoidance: one fact per page, a named feeling, a visible tear, and then a ritual that lets the child do something (in the grandmother book, as I recall, visiting the grave with flowers).
- The "first experience" list is effectively the series bible for Spot, Miffy and Maisy and is how the series extends indefinitely without needing plot.

#### Gaps
- UNVERIFIED RECALL: Dear Grandma Bunny (Dutch 1996) shows the grandmother in her coffin and Miffy crying, then the grave being tended; and Miffy in Hospital covers a tonsil operation with an injection and a present afterwards. I could not open a source describing the page content.
- Titles for Miffy at the seaside and their dates were not confirmed in this session (the zoo, hospital, new baby and grandmother titles are confirmed to exist).
- No critical source opened discusses how Spot handles a difficult subject; the new-sibling book (1989) is the nearest.

### 7. What the makers and their editors have said about rules and method

#### Takeaway
Bruna worked by reduction under self-imposed constraints; Hill started from a mechanism and a respect for children's intelligence; Carle started from a physical idea in a 32-page dummy and relied on one editor's two interventions (change the animal; find a printer).

#### Cited Findings
- Bruna: "No line is redundant"; he called his approach the art of omission and wanted to leave room for the reader's imagination. — [DesignCurial](https://old.designcurial.com/news/miffy-creator-dick-bruna---the-art-of-reduction-270115-4497133/)
- Bruna on process: he searches for a long time, "throwing lots away", before a moment of recognition; he drew real subjects (an elephant at the zoo) and then removed detail. — [DesignCurial](https://old.designcurial.com/news/miffy-creator-dick-bruna---the-art-of-reduction-270115-4497133/)
- Bruna's routine: for about fifty years he cycled to his Utrecht studio for about 8 am, lunch at noon, work until 6 pm; alone, by hand. — [DesignCurial](https://old.designcurial.com/news/miffy-creator-dick-bruna---the-art-of-reduction-270115-4497133/)
- Bruna attributed quote (snippet, secondary): putting very few things on a page leaves lots of room for the imagination. — [Lateral Action: Miffy](https://lateralaction.com/articles/miffy/)
- Bruna on imitation (about Hello Kitty): "Try to make something that you think of yourself." — [Wikipedia: Miffy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy), citing The Daily Telegraph
- Institutional home: the dick bruna huis opened at the Centraal Museum, Utrecht, in 2006 (now the nijntje museum); the Rijksmuseum holds 120 Bruna works on loan. — [Wikipedia: Miffy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy); [DesignCurial](https://old.designcurial.com/news/miffy-creator-dick-bruna---the-art-of-reduction-270115-4497133/)
- Hill: aimed to recognise that children "have far more intelligence and style than many adults credit them with". — [Wikipedia: Eric Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Hill)
- Hill made a handmade dummy first; the mechanism preceded the story. — [Publishers Weekly obituary](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/obituaries/article/62808-obituary-eric-hill.html)
- Carle: the idea comes first; he sketches it into a 32-page dummy, sometimes many times; "It's hard work and you have to have faith in yourself." A child once called him a "picture writer". — [eric-carle.com FAQ](https://eric-carle.com/faq/)
- Carle on editing: a book is "like a bridge" from writer to reader, and the editor checks it for rough spots. — [eric-carle.com FAQ](https://eric-carle.com/faq/)
- Once the caterpillar and butterfly were settled, Carle finished the book in about two days. — [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind)
- Carle disliked the 1993 animated adaptation, calling it "godawful". — [Wikipedia: The Very Hungry Caterpillar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Very_Hungry_Caterpillar)
- Cousins: her style of black outline and bright flat colour was developed at art college and feels "comfortable and natural" (snippet). — [Carle Museum blog](https://carlemuseum.org/blogs/making-art/lucy-cousins-painted-our-studio)

#### Inferences
- Bruna's rules, assembled from the cited sources: square; 12 openings; picture right, text left; four lines, one rhyme; hand-drawn black brush line; a small fixed set of flat colours; one subject per picture; nothing that is not needed. Every rule reduces choice for both maker and child.
- In all three cases an editor or agent made a decisive structural contribution (Beneduce's caterpillar; Boase's animal-for-child swap on Maisy; the dummy route to Frankfurt for Hill). The books were designed as objects first and manuscripts second.

#### Gaps
- No direct Mercis or Centraal Museum text could be opened (miffy.com 403). The estate's current stated rules for use of the character were not found.
- UNVERIFIED RECALL: Bruna reportedly made around a hundred drawings for a 12-picture book and took months per title; a targeted search did not surface a source for the number.
- UNVERIFIED RECALL: Carle's often-quoted statement that the first day of school is a great trauma and that his books are meant to bridge home and school was not found on the FAQ page opened; the FAQ's "bridge" quote is about writer and reader.
- No Rod Campbell interview could be opened (Bookseller feature paywalled).

### 8. Sales, reach, and why they last

#### Takeaway
All figures are publisher or estate claims repeated by the press and should be quoted as "more than" with a date. Critics and educators credit the same few things: predictability and rhythm, something for the child's hands to do, graphic clarity, and an emotionally safe ending.

#### Cited Findings
- Miffy: more than 85 million copies and more than 50 languages (BBC, 2013). Wikipedia's lead says over 100 million while its body says 85 million; one snippet says over 80 million. — [BBC report reposted on Beattie's Book Blog](https://beattiesbookblog.blogspot.com/2013/08/miffy-books-to-be-updated-for-modern.html); [Wikipedia: Miffy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miffy)
- Spot: more than 60 million copies (Kirkus, Philadelphia Inquirer, Wikipedia) versus more than 50 million copies and 65 languages (Publishers Weekly); 60 languages elsewhere. — [Kirkus](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/news-and-features/articles/memoriam-eric-hill-1927-2014); [Philadelphia Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/philly/obituaries/20140615_Eric_Hill____Where_s_Spot___author__86.html); [Publishers Weekly](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/obituaries/article/62808-obituary-eric-hill.html)
- The Very Hungry Caterpillar: more than 50 million copies worldwide (Penguin article body) or more than 55 million and 70 languages (Carle's official biography as quoted by Penguin); roughly one copy sold every 30 seconds according to the publisher; 60+ or 70+ languages depending on source. — [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind); [Wikipedia: The Very Hungry Caterpillar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Very_Hungry_Caterpillar)
- Penguin's claim that the average UK child reads it about nine times a year. — [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind)
- Recognition: AIGA award 1970; New York Times Ten Best Illustrated 1969; No. 2 in School Library Journal's Top 100 Picture Books poll (2012); tenth most borrowed book in New York Public Library history (2020). — [Wikipedia: The Very Hungry Caterpillar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Very_Hungry_Caterpillar)
- Dear Zoo: 13 million copies according to Macmillan, of which 2.5 million are recorded by Nielsen BookScan (snippet); older sources say 8 million or 2 million. — [The Bookseller](https://thebookseller.com/features/macmillan-marks-dear-zoo-milestone)
- Maisy: more than 28 million copies, 29 languages (Wikipedia); "more than 30 million books in print" for Cousins overall (Publishers Weekly, snippet). — [Wikipedia: Maisy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maisy_(book_series)); [Publishers Weekly Q&A with Lucy Cousins](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/interviews/article/54137-q-a-with-lucy-cousins.html)
- Why they last, as stated by sources: for Carle, rhythm and repetition, layered learning under a simple surface, the hole as performance, and an ending about growing up (Leonard Marcus and Penguin); for Hill, the flap as hide-and-seek and respect for children's intelligence; for Bruna, reduction that leaves space for imagination and a format children can hold themselves; for Campbell, the anticipation of the flap. — [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/discover/articles/making-eric-carle-very-hungry-caterpillar-story-behind); [Wikipedia: Eric Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Hill); [DesignCurial](https://old.designcurial.com/news/miffy-creator-dick-bruna---the-art-of-reduction-270115-4497133/); [1001 Stories](https://1001stories.co.uk/stories/dear-zoo)

#### Inferences
- The Nielsen-versus-publisher gap on Dear Zoo (2.5 million tracked UK sales against a 13 million global claim) is a useful reminder of how soft all the headline numbers are; the same caution applies to the 85/100 million and 50/60 million ranges.
- Transferable craft principles for an original series (structure only, nothing copyable in itself): one engine per book, visible by the second opening; a fixed container reused across titles; a single physical or graphic action for the child on each opening; five to eight pattern beats then one break; a lead with minimal stated personality and a secure home to return to; flat colour, heavy line, empty backgrounds; text short enough to be memorised and "read back" by a non-reader; hard subjects handled inside the normal format rather than in a special one.
- What is distinctive and should not be imitated: Bruna's exact face construction and colour set, Hill's dog-behind-furniture search, Carle's eaten-holes-through-food device and painted-tissue look, Campbell's crates-from-the-zoo conceit.

#### Gaps
- No independent (non-publisher) worldwide sales audit exists in the sources found for any of the five.
- Books for Keeps, Horn Book and Guardian criticism could not be opened in this session, so the "why they last" evidence is thinner on independent scholarship than intended; Leonard Marcus (via Penguin) is the only named critic cited.
- The Wikipedia summary returned to me said "over 55 million copies sold in the US"; I believe this is a summarising error for worldwide and have reported it as worldwide per Penguin.

## Donaldson and Scheffler, Mr. Men and Little Miss, and Richard Scarry: how three kinds of picture book are built

Items under "Inferences" are the researcher's reading of that evidence. ### 1. Donaldson and Scheffler: The Gruffalo, Zog and the rhyming story

#### Takeaway
A Donaldson and Scheffler book is a 32-page, roughly 700 to 800 word song without music: rhyming couplets in a strong, bouncing metre, a repeated encounter with a refrain, a pattern of three, and a late twist, with Scheffler's pictures supplying setting, comedy and character that the compressed verse leaves out. Donaldson came to books from songwriting and treats rhyme as a tool for concision, not decoration.

#### Cited Findings

Length and format
- The Gruffalo was published 23 March 1999 by Macmillan Children's Books, has 32 pages and is "about 700 words long" — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)
- A catalogue entry gives a precise word count of 687 for The Gruffalo — [TeachingBooks](https://library.teachingbooks.net/tb.cgi?tid=9121) (catalogue figure, as reported in a search result; not counted independently)
- Zog was published in 2010 by Alison Green Books (Scholastic), 32 pages, unpaginated — [Wikipedia, Zog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zog_(children%27s_book))
- A catalogue entry gives 812 words for Zog (US title A Gold Star for Zog), listed for grades PK to 2 — [TeachingBooks](https://library.teachingbooks.net/tb.cgi?tid=30963); a second listing gives about 25 words per page and a reading age of 4 to 8 — [Bookroo](https://bookroo.com/books/a-gold-star-for-zog)
- The Gruffalo was written in about two weeks plus rewriting — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)

Verse form, metre, rhyme
- The Gruffalo is in rhyming couplets, "primarily dactylic tetrameter" (one stressed syllable followed by two unstressed), described as a relatively uncommon metre — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)
- The rhythm is deliberately broken at key moments: a pause when the mouse names the meeting place, and an exclamation when he first sees the real creature — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)
- Rhymes mix predictable pairs (mouse/house) with less predictable ones; alliteration starts in the opening line ("deep, dark wood"); one adjective ("terrible") recurs for each of the creature's features; concrete nouns dominate over abstract ones — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo), summarising Burke (2022)
- A 2021 stylistics paper in a SAGE journal analyses the book's rhythm, rhyme and lexical repetition (only seen via a search result; full text not read) — [SAGE, doi 10.1177/09639470211072162](https://doi.org/10.1177/09639470211072162)

Encounter structure, rule of three, twist
- The mouse meets three predators in turn (fox, owl, snake); each invites him to eat as a trick; each time he invents and describes a monster and scares the predator off — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)
- The twist: the invented monster turns out to be real and wants to eat him; the mouse then walks back past the same three predators in reverse, who flee, convincing the monster that the mouse is the frightening one — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)
- The book is classed as a trickster tale; Donaldson has said it was inspired by a Chinese folk tale about a fox borrowing the terror of a tiger. She intended a tiger but could not find rhymes for it, so invented a three-syllable name that would rhyme; rejected names included "snargle" and "tiglophant" — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)
- The setting never changes and the final picture mirrors the opening — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)
- Scholarship cited there says three to five year olds respond mainly to the surprise and repetition, six to eight year olds to rhyme, rhythm and exaggeration (van der Westhuizen 2007) — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)

Zog: school and a character who fails
- Zog is a young, accident-prone dragon who wants to be the best pupil at dragon school — [Wikipedia, Zog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zog_(children%27s_book))
- He learns new skills with enthusiasm at Madam Dragon's school but repeatedly falls, bumps or burns himself; a girl appears each time to tend the injury and his pride; he eventually realises his training is leading to capturing a princess, which sets up the climax — [Books for Keeps review, issue 187, March 2011, Judith Sharman](https://booksforkeeps.co.uk/review/zog/)
- Books for Keeps rates it for under-fives (pre-school, nursery, infant) and calls the verse lively and the pictures humorous with a note of anxiety that builds suspense — [Books for Keeps](https://booksforkeeps.co.uk/review/zog/)
- Zog won the Galaxy National Book Award in 2010; sequel Zog and the Flying Doctors, 2016 — [Wikipedia, Zog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zog_(children%27s_book))

What Donaldson says about rhyme, rhythm, songs and reading aloud
- She wrote songs and children's plays before picture books; rhyme is not a deliberate "picture book" choice but comes from songwriting, and she does not always use it — [Publishers Weekly Q&A](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/interviews/article/47027-q-a-with-julia-donaldson-and-axel-scheffler.html)
- Her view: bad rhyme makes a story drag; good rhyme lets her be concise and avoid describing everything; a repeated refrain gives a text a ballad-like shape — [Publishers Weekly Q&A](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/interviews/article/47027-q-a-with-julia-donaldson-and-axel-scheffler.html)
- The Gruffalo grew out of adapting traditional tales into short plays; one publisher stalled it and an editor suggested rewriting it in prose; she took it elsewhere and it was accepted with no text changes — [Publishers Weekly Q&A](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/interviews/article/47027-q-a-with-julia-donaldson-and-axel-scheffler.html)
- She does not write for a set age; the vocabulary must be concise — [Publishers Weekly Q&A](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/interviews/article/47027-q-a-with-julia-donaldson-and-axel-scheffler.html)
- She told the story aloud on many school visits before and after publication and children drew their own versions of the creature — [Publishers Weekly Q&A](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/interviews/article/47027-q-a-with-julia-donaldson-and-axel-scheffler.html)
- "Children love rhyme and rhythm, it's a very natural thing"; she ties this to nursery rhymes and chants, and holds that rhyme without rhythm is pointless — [Tes interview](https://www.tes.com/news/get-kids-reading-secret-you-know-turn-terrible-gruffalo)
- As Children's Laureate she campaigned for singing as well as reading; BookTrust notes she came to books through songwriting and that the sound of the words matters as much as the sense — [BookTrust, Sing as well as read](https://www.booktrust.org.uk/books/childrens-laureate/former-laureates/julia-donaldson/sing-as-well-as-read/)
- The Gruffalo won the Blue Peter award for Best Book to Read Aloud and the Nestle Smarties Prize — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)

What Scheffler says about design and hidden details
- His first sketches showed a humanoid, troll-like creature in T-shirt and trousers; editor Alison Green and Donaldson wanted a woodland animal — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)
- The editor judged early versions too scary for small children, so he made the creature rounder and more cuddly; he had planned to dress all the animals but Donaldson objected, and he was relieved (how would one dress a snake?) — [Picturebook Makers, Axel Scheffler](https://blog.picturebookmakers.com/post/axel-scheffler)
- He added features not in the text (for example cow-like horns); the monster becomes progressively more of a buffoon across the book; it is pictured nine times — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)
- Getting the scale between mouse and monster right was hard; he did not want the monster on the cover but the publisher insisted; about twelve alternative covers were drawn — [Publishers Weekly Q&A](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/interviews/article/47027-q-a-with-julia-donaldson-and-axel-scheffler.html); [Picturebook Makers](https://blog.picturebookmakers.com/post/axel-scheffler)
- Foreign co-edition editors sometimes ask for no teeth or claws; he calls himself an "obedient illustrator" — [Publishers Weekly Q&A](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/interviews/article/47027-q-a-with-julia-donaldson-and-axel-scheffler.html)
- Since The Snail and the Whale he has hidden a small Gruffalo in every book with Donaldson (except The Ugly Five) — [Picturebook Makers](https://blog.picturebookmakers.com/post/axel-scheffler); examples: a Christmas tree decoration in Stick Man, a child's drawing in The Snail and the Whale, a fish in Tiddler — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)
- Technique: small sketches enlarged, traced on a lightbox onto watercolour paper, dip-pen black outline, liquid inks, coloured pencil on top, white gouache highlights — [Picturebook Makers](https://blog.picturebookmakers.com/post/axel-scheffler)

How text and picture divide the work
- Donaldson writes alone, with no input from editor or illustrator, but thinks about what the illustrator will need to draw; she sees nothing until roughs arrive; the two communicate mainly through the editor — [Publishers Weekly Q&A](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/interviews/article/47027-q-a-with-julia-donaldson-and-axel-scheffler.html)
- Donaldson credits Scheffler with adding inventive, funny details beyond the words — [Publishers Weekly Q&A](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/interviews/article/47027-q-a-with-julia-donaldson-and-axel-scheffler.html)
- The forest was based on woods near Hamburg — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)

Sales
- Over 13.5 million copies of The Gruffalo sold by 2019; translated into more than 100 languages and dialects — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo) (secondary figure; not checked against the publisher)

#### Inferences
- 32 pages with a title page and endpapers leaves about 12 double-page spreads for story. At roughly 690 to 810 words that is 55 to 70 words a spread, or four to six couplets, which is about the amount an adult can read in one breath-group before a page turn.
- The Gruffalo's shape is three encounters, a turn, then the same three encounters in reverse order (a mirror or "there and back" structure). The repetition lets a child predict and join in; the reversal lets the same material carry the pay-off, so almost nothing new has to be introduced after the midpoint.
- The description of the monster is assembled feature by feature across the three encounters, so the refrain does two jobs: it is the join-in line and it is the character brief the picture later confirms. The reveal spread works because the child has already been told what to look for.
- Breaking the metre at the point of surprise is a technique worth noting: a steady beat makes any deviation felt, so the verse itself signals the turn.
- Zog swaps the journey for a calendar: a recurring lesson, a recurring failure, a recurring helper. The pattern makes failure safe because the child knows help is coming, and the pay-off is that the helper, not the hero's eventual competence, solves the final problem.
- The hidden creature in later books is a retention device for the adult and child across a whole list, not just within one book; it rewards owning several titles.
- The editor's interventions (less scary, no clothes, creature on the cover) show that silhouette and tone were decided by committee against the illustrator's first instincts, and that the "cuddly but scary" balance is the product of revision.
- Age: the read-aloud works from about 2.5 to 3; the trick (understanding that the mouse is lying and that the monster misreads why the others run) needs some grasp of false belief and is more fully enjoyed from 4. Zog's school setting and longer text place it at 3 to 4 and up.
- What it asks of the adult: a performance. The metre has to be kept, voices differentiated, and pauses held before the rhyme word so the child can supply it. A poor reader of verse is exposed more than with prose.

#### Gaps
- UNVERIFIED (researcher's recollection of the book, no source located this session; one attempted source was unreachable): Zog's year-by-year plan is flying in year one, roaring in year two, breathing fire in year three, capturing a princess in year four and fighting in year five; each year the teacher promises a golden star; the helping girl turns out to be a princess who volunteers to be captured so Zog wins his star; she, the knight sent to rescue her and Zog then leave to become travelling doctors. The report writer should check a copy before relying on the year-by-year detail.
- UNVERIFIED: structures of Room on the Broom (cumulative: three animals each find a dropped item and ask for a place, then combine at the climax) and Stick Man (a chain of mistaken identities with a repeated protest refrain, seasonal arc, rescue by Father Christmas). No source was fetched for these.
- Word counts (687 and 812) come from one catalogue each and were not independently counted. "About 700" for The Gruffalo is the safer statement.
- The dactylic tetrameter description comes from Wikipedia summarising Burke; a line-by-line scansion was not seen. Some analysts hear the line as anapaestic with a dropped opening; either way it is a triple (three-syllable) foot with four beats.
- No direct Donaldson quote on "the rule of three" was found.
- Sales figures after 2019 were not found.

### 2. Mr. Men and Little Miss: one character, one trait, one small book

#### Takeaway
The series is a rigid template built by an advertising copywriter: a small square paperback, a character who is a name, a colour, a shape and a single trait, and a short story in which that trait causes trouble. The characters work like logos, which explains both their durability and the criticism that they reduce people (and especially girls) to labels.

#### Cited Findings

Format and formula
- Each book has a title page, 16 to 18 pages of text and 15 to 17 full-colour illustrations facing them, drawn with a Magic Marker; paperback, 14 cm by 12.6 cm — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men)
- Each book introduces one title character with one dominant personality trait and a simple moral; characters often reappear in other books — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men)
- The spines of each series line up to form a slogan and a picture (a worm for Mr. Men, a flower for Little Miss) — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men)
- Illustrations are brightly coloured and boldly drawn; text set in plain sans-serif faces (Univers, Helvetica, Optima) — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men)

Origin and author
- The first six books were published in the UK on 10 August 1971; the first was Mr. Tickle — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men)
- Origin story: Hargreaves's son Adam, aged about eight, asked what a tickle looked like; the answer was a round figure with a small blue hat, very long arms and a big smile — [New Statesman, Amelia Tait, 18 Aug 2021](https://www.newstatesman.com/culture/books/2021/08/fifty-years-of-mr-men-books); the story is disputed, with another account saying the figures began as doodles at work — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men)
- Hargreaves had worked in advertising for more than 13 years — [New Statesman](https://www.newstatesman.com/culture/books/2021/08/fifty-years-of-mr-men-books); the official site describes him as a successful advertising copywriter — [mrmen.com, About us](https://www.mrmen.com/pages/about-us)
- Hargreaves said he could not draw well and so kept things simple, and that short stories suited him because he was "much too lazy to write a novel" (reported via search result; original interview not seen) — [Lambiek Comiclopedia, Roger Hargreaves](https://www.lambiek.net/artists/h/hargreaves_roger.htm)
- Mr. Tickle sold a million copies in its first three years — [mrmen.com](https://www.mrmen.com/pages/about-us)

World and crossing between books
- Stories take place in a fictional land called Misterland, also named Dillydale in later material; besides the title characters it holds ordinary humans (shopkeepers, doctors, postmen) and animals; a worm called Walter recurs most — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men)

Size of the series and extension
- Hargreaves wrote 38 more Mr. Men in the 1970s; the Little Miss series began in 1981 with Little Miss Bossy; he created 21 Little Miss characters; he died in 1988 — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men); [mrmen.com](https://www.mrmen.com/pages/about-us)
- As of 2021, 92 Mr. Men and Little Miss characters — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men); "over 90 characters" — [New Statesman](https://www.newstatesman.com/culture/books/2021/08/fifty-years-of-mr-men-books)
- Adam Hargreaves began writing and drawing new titles in 2003; he says it took about three years to learn to draw the characters; books still carry his father's name — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men)
- New characters by public vote in 2021 (Mr. Calm, Little Miss Brave); 2016 additions (Mr. Marvelous, Mr. Adventure, Little Miss Fabulous, Little Miss Sparkle); Little Miss Inventor; licensed tie-ins with Doctor Who (2017) and the Spice Girls (2019); brand characters for Evian and La Mer — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men); [New Statesman](https://www.newstatesman.com/culture/books/2021/08/fifty-years-of-mr-men-books)
- A 2022 "Discover You" series uses the characters to talk about feelings and wellbeing; the same year a "Little Miss" meme used the template for ironic adult self-description — [mrmen.com](https://www.mrmen.com/pages/about-us); [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men)
- TV: BBC series from 1974 narrated by Arthur Lowe; Little Miss series 1983 — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men)
- Rights sold to Chorion in 2004 for £28 million; acquired by Sanrio in 2011; UK publisher Egmont 1998 to 2021, then Farshore (HarperCollins) — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men)

Sales
- "More than 250 million copies" worldwide — [New Statesman](https://www.newstatesman.com/culture/books/2021/08/fifty-years-of-mr-men-books); "over 250 million copies across 28 countries" — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men). Older or other sources give 100 million and "over 85 million" — [Hachette Australia](https://www.hachette.com.au/roger-hargreaves/); [Toppsta](https://toppsta.com/authors/profile/roger-hargreaves). Figures conflict by date and source; treat 250 million as the rights-holder's current claim, not an audited number.

Criticism
- A 2018 University of Lincoln study (Madeleine Pownall) analysed 47 Mr. Men and 34 Little Miss books (1971 to 2014): Little Miss characters had to be rescued by another character in 51.5% of stories against 32.6% for Mr. Men, and male characters had more dialogue — [Yorkshire Post, 8 May 2018](https://www.pressreader.com/uk/yorkshire-post/20180508/281792809659729); [The New Daily](https://thenewdaily.com.au/entertainment/books/2018/05/08/mr-men-sexist-gender-stereotypes/)
- The peer-reviewed follow-up (Pownall and Heflick, Sex Roles, 2023) found male characters more adventurous and active, female characters more domestic and passive, and trait names tracking feminine and masculine stereotypes; parents preferred counter-stereotypical books for daughters — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/207093/)
- The same coverage cites the name Little Miss Bossy and a story ending in which a female character goes to cook and clean for a male one; a politician objected that "Little Miss" implies "less than" — [Irish News, 9 May 2018](https://www.irishnews.com/magazine/entertainment/2018/05/09/news/emily-thornberry-little-miss-books-imply-women-are-less-than-mr-men-1325821/)
- Frank Cottrell-Boyce is reported as calling the books "democratic" and like early-reader school books (via search summary of the New Statesman piece; the fetched text did not show this passage) — [New Statesman](https://www.newstatesman.com/culture/books/2021/08/fifty-years-of-mr-men-books)
- The brand describes its appeal as self-expression, colour, simplicity and humour — [mrmen.com](https://www.mrmen.com/pages/about-us)

#### Inferences
- The page plan (text on one page, one picture facing it, 16 to 18 times) is the simplest possible picture-book grammar: one statement, one image. There is no visual subplot and almost no background; the picture restates the sentence. This is the opposite of both Scheffler and Scarry.
- The character is a complete design system: name equals trait equals plot premise; colour plus basic geometric shape plus one accessory (hat, shoes, bow, bandages) equals identity. Each can be recognised as a flat silhouette at thumbnail size, which is why they license so easily.
- How a trait becomes a plot, in practice, falls into a small number of moves: the trait is demonstrated in escalating examples; then either it is cured by an outside agent (a wizard, a doctor, a trick), or it is punished, or it is found a use (the trait is the right fit for one job), or it simply persists and the book ends on a wink. A new series can use the "find the trait a use" ending without the correction endings that critics dislike.
- The risk the criticism identifies is structural, not incidental: when the name is the trait, the character cannot change without ceasing to be the character, and a child may hear a trait name as a verdict. A cast defined by what characters like doing or are good at, rather than by a fault, avoids this.
- The small format (roughly palm-sized and cheap) made the books collectable and child-owned; the numbered list and the spine picture turn completion into the goal.
- Age: the pictures are readable from 2, but the texts are wordier than they look and the humour is verbal; best at 3 to 5 and as early independent reading at 5 to 7.
- What it asks of the adult: a storyteller's dry, confiding voice. The texts are prose with no rhyme to carry them, so the comedy depends on timing and deadpan delivery.

#### Gaps
- UNVERIFIED (researcher's recollection, no source found this session): the narrator is an intrusive, chatty "I" who addresses the reader directly with asides and questions, uses very short paragraphs and one-line pay-offs, invents nonsense place names and numbers, and often ends by turning to the child with a question or warning. Mr. Happy lives in "Happyland" and several other books have their own named lands (for example Coldland, Nonsenseland), with "Misterland" as the later umbrella name. Two searches failed to surface a source naming Happyland; the report writer should check the books.
- Page count: sources give 16 to 18 text pages plus facing pictures, which implies about 32 to 36 pages in total; an exact total was not confirmed.
- Word count: no reliable figure found. Researcher's estimate from familiarity with the books is several hundred words, commonly put at 500 to 900, and is UNVERIFIED.
- No scholarly close reading of the narrative voice was found; most coverage is about brand, sales and the gender debate.
- The total number of titles (as opposed to characters), including specials and tie-ins, was not established.

### 3. Richard Scarry: the busy spread and the book as a place

#### Takeaway
A Scarry book is a large-format, long, crowded reference world, not a story: labelled pictures arranged as scenes, animals doing every human job, small comic accidents everywhere, and a few recurring characters (plus one tiny hidden one) to follow from spread to spread. The child reads it by pointing, naming, searching and coming back, and the books were visibly revised to fix their 1960s gender roles.

#### Cited Findings

Scale and career
- Scarry (1919 to 1994) published over 300 books with total sales over 100 million — [Wikipedia, Richard Scarry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Scarry) (secondary figure)
- He illustrated Golden Books from 1949; Best Word Book Ever first appeared in 1963; Busy, Busy World 1965; Storybook Dictionary 1967; What Do People Do All Day? 1968; Cars and Trucks and Things That Go 1974 — [Wikipedia, Richard Scarry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Scarry); [Yale Review, Chris Ware, "Writing in Pictures"](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry); [Literary Hub](https://lithub.com/why-are-so-many-adults-still-obsessed-with-busytown/)
- Best Word Book Ever sold about seven million copies by 1975 — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry)

Format and page counts
- Cars and Trucks and Things That Go: 72 pages — [Penguin listing](https://www.penguin.com.au/books/cars-and-trucks-and-things-that-go-9780307157850) (one Goodreads edition lists 69)
- What Do People Do All Day?: 96 pages in the current hardback — [National Building Museum shop](https://nbm.org/product/what-do-people-do-all-day/) (abridged editions exist; original length not confirmed)
- The word books are large-format and dense, with many small labelled scenes per page; content is organised by double-page spread on a theme (a morning routine, a mealtime) — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry)

How the spread works
- Chris Ware describes the method as "images-as-words": the pictures act as a dictionary and the labels invite exploration — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry)
- An editor's line quoted there: Scarry "didn't write his stories; he drew them" — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry)
- In Cars and Trucks the traffic moves left to right, matching reading direction and the page turn — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry)
- The books mostly avoid plot; a newspaper description quoted by Literary Hub says the reader is "hanging out in a world" more than following a story — [Literary Hub](https://lithub.com/why-are-so-many-adults-still-obsessed-with-busytown/)
- Animals do human jobs, each with a simple caption (a fox flying a plane, a pig laying bricks) — [Literary Hub](https://lithub.com/why-are-so-many-adults-still-obsessed-with-busytown/)

Recurring cast and the thing to find
- Named recurring characters include Huckle Cat, Lowly Worm, Sergeant Murphy, Bananas Gorilla, Mr. Frumble, Mr. Fixit — [Wikipedia, Richard Scarry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Scarry)
- Lowly Worm wears a green hat and a single shoe and drives a car made from an apple; Scarry counted him a favourite and believed children like meeting familiar characters again, as they like familiar people — [Literary Hub](https://lithub.com/why-are-so-many-adults-still-obsessed-with-busytown/)
- Goldbug, a tiny gold insect, is hidden on nearly every page of Cars and Trucks so the child keeps looking; publisher copy invites children to search each spread for him — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry); [Penguin Random House listing](https://penguinrandomhouse.ca/books/741716/richard-scarrys-cars-and-trucks-and-things-that-go-by-written-and-illustrated-by-richard-scarry/9780593706305)

How children read them
- Reading is active: pointing at objects, acting out scenes, searching for hidden details; Ware recalls copying a bear's morning routine from the book as a small child — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry)
- Publisher copy describes generations of children "poring over" the detail of daily life — [Penguin Random House](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/742173/richard-scarrys-cars-and-trucks-fold-and-find-by-richard-scarry/9780593807675/)

Why animals; Scarry's stance
- Ware argues children empathise readily with animals and that giving animals human lives is a way of showing care; the publisher once queried whether a book about "people" should say "animals" — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry)
- Scarry reportedly did not test books on children because he had stayed childlike himself — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry)
- His mature style (loose pencil line, doodle-like, animal cast) arrived with Best Word Book Ever, after years in the more painterly Golden Books house style — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry)
- His world is described as having "a strong sense of community and cooperation" (Orlando Sentinel, 1995, quoted) — [Wikipedia, Richard Scarry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Scarry)

Humour and small disasters; criticism
- An academic reading of What Do People Do All Day? counts the accidents and finds pigs involved in, and causing, far more than other animals, and argues the book teaches a class structure — [Literary Hub](https://lithub.com/why-are-so-many-adults-still-obsessed-with-busytown/)
- The books are also criticised for rigid traditional gender roles and some racially insensitive depictions in early editions — [Literary Hub](https://lithub.com/why-are-so-many-adults-still-obsessed-with-busytown/)

Revisions
- Best Word Book Ever (1963) was revised; a page-by-page photo comparison of the 1963 and 1991 printings by Alan Taylor is the standard source — [Kottke](https://kottke.org/15/11/a-more-progressive-richard-scarry); [Upworthy](https://www.upworthy.com/8-changes-that-were-made-to-a-classic-richard-scarry-book-to-keep-up-with-the-times-progress)
- Documented changes: a father added to the kitchen beside the mother; a label describing a "beautiful screaming lady" being rescued changed to "cat in danger"; job titles made gender-neutral (a "pretty stewardess" becomes a flight attendant); a Hanukkah menorah added to a holiday page that had only Christmas and Halloween — [Kottke](https://kottke.org/15/11/a-more-progressive-richard-scarry); [Upworthy](https://www.upworthy.com/8-changes-that-were-made-to-a-classic-richard-scarry-book-to-keep-up-with-the-times-progress)
- Date conflict: Wikipedia gives the revision as 1980 — [Wikipedia, Richard Scarry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Scarry); the comparison articles use a 1991 printing — [Kottke](https://kottke.org/15/11/a-more-progressive-richard-scarry). Likely a 1980 revised edition with further changes visible by the 1991 printing, but this was not resolved.
- A 2024 50th-anniversary edition of Cars and Trucks has an afterword by Scarry's son on how the book was made — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry)

What makes them last (Ware)
- Detail that communicates what words cannot; a child's-eye view; warmth and a cooperative civic world; and simple repeated pleasures (recognising characters, finding the bug) that pull the child back — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry)

#### Inferences
- The unit of design is the spread, not the story. Each spread is a self-contained themed place (kitchen, airport, building site, road) with dozens of entry points, so the book can be opened anywhere and "read" for two minutes or twenty.
- Three layers run at once on a spread: labels (naming), micro-incidents (a hat blowing off, a spill, a pile-up) that are tiny wordless stories, and a through-line (a family's journey, a recurring pursuit, the hidden bug) that gives a reason to turn the page. A child can use whichever layer matches their age.
- The hidden character converts a reference book into a game with a win condition on every spread, and gives a pre-reader a task they can do better than the adult.
- Small disasters are safe slapstick: nobody is hurt, everything is put right by a competent neighbour. The implied message is that mishaps are normal and communities fix things.
- Jobs are the cast-management system: each character is species plus costume plus vehicle plus occupation, so dozens can appear on one spread without confusion and without needing names.
- The revisions show the cost of a world built from roles: every label and every job assignment is a statement about who does what, so a job-based cast needs a deliberate spread of roles across genders (and species) from the start.
- Age: the widest range of the three. Pointing and naming from about 18 months; searching and micro-stories at 2 to 4; the process spreads (how a house or a road is built, how bread or paper is made) at 4 to 6.
- What it asks of the adult: conversation, not performance. There is little to read aloud straight through; the adult asks "where is...?", "what is he doing?", names things and follows the child's finger. It is poor as a quick bedtime read and strong as a shared lap book or solo browsing book.

#### Gaps
- Page count and trim size of Best Word Book Ever and of the original (unabridged) What Do People Do All Day? were not confirmed. The often-quoted claim that Best Word Book Ever labels more than 1,400 objects was not found in a fetched source and is UNVERIFIED.
- No direct quotation from Scarry about making books "fun first, educational second" was located, though the idea is widely attributed to him.
- UNVERIFIED (researcher's recollection): Cars and Trucks has a through-line of a pig family's drive to a picnic and a running chase involving a police officer and a reckless driver; Sergeant Murphy rides a motorcycle and Bananas Gorilla steals bananas. Sources fetched name these characters but do not describe those roles.
- The Horn Book and Guardian were searched for but no Scarry piece from either was retrieved.
- The academic paper on class and accidents was seen only through Literary Hub's summary; author and journal not confirmed.

### 4. Age fit, the adult's job, and why each lasts (side by side)

#### Takeaway
The three models suit different moments in the 0 to 5 span and ask different things of the adult: Scarry is for pointing and talking from the second year, Donaldson and Scheffler for performed story from about three, Mr. Men for dry, spoken comedy from three and into early independent reading.

#### Cited Findings
- Books for Keeps places Zog in its under-fives band (pre-school, nursery, infant) — [Books for Keeps](https://booksforkeeps.co.uk/review/zog/); US listings put it at grades PK to 2 or ages 4 to 8 — [TeachingBooks](https://library.teachingbooks.net/tb.cgi?tid=30963); [Bookroo](https://bookroo.com/books/a-gold-star-for-zog)
- The Gruffalo is described as written for children who cannot yet read or are learning, to be read aloud; three to five year olds respond to surprise and repetition, older children to rhyme, rhythm and exaggeration — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)
- Donaldson: rhyme and rhythm are natural to children raised on nursery rhymes, and parents tell her they sing the books — [Tes](https://www.tes.com/news/get-kids-reading-secret-you-know-turn-terrible-gruffalo); [BookTrust](https://www.booktrust.org.uk/books/childrens-laureate/former-laureates/julia-donaldson/sing-as-well-as-read/)
- Mr. Men: likened to early-reader school books and called "democratic" (Cottrell-Boyce, as reported) — [New Statesman](https://www.newstatesman.com/culture/books/2021/08/fifty-years-of-mr-men-books); brand cites colour, simplicity, humour — [mrmen.com](https://www.mrmen.com/pages/about-us)
- Scarry: active reading by pointing, acting out and searching; repeated small pleasures bring children back — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry)

#### Inferences
- Summary table (researcher's synthesis):

| | Donaldson and Scheffler | Mr. Men and Little Miss | Scarry's Busytown |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core unit | The line and the refrain | The character | The spread |
| Extent | 32 pages, about 700 to 800 words | About 32 to 36 small pages, prose (word count unverified) | 72 to 96 large pages, labels and captions |
| Best age in 0 to 5 | 3 to 5 (listenable from 2.5) | 3 to 5, then early solo reading | 1.5 to 5, widest span |
| Adult's job | Perform the verse, hold the beat, pause for the rhyme | Tell it deadpan, like a joke | Talk, ask, name, follow the finger |
| Picture's job | Adds setting, comedy, sub-detail beyond the text | Restates the sentence | Is the content |

| Main weakness | Hard to write well; bad verse is worse than prose | Flat labels; dated gender split | Needs time and an engaged adult; role stereotypes |

- All three last for the same underlying reason: a strict, visible pattern the child can master (a refrain, a template, a search) combined with something the adult enjoys doing.

- No educator study directly comparing these three formats was found; the age bands above combine publisher and review listings with the researcher's judgement.
- Nothing here establishes suitability for under-18-month children; none of the three was designed as a baby book (board-book abridgements exist but were not researched).

### 5. Keeping a large cast distinct

#### Takeaway
Each creator solves the big-cast problem with a different primary hook: Hargreaves by name-as-trait plus colour and shape, Scarry by species plus job plus vehicle (with a handful of named regulars), and Donaldson and Scheffler by keeping each book's cast tiny and letting verse, a repeated line and a consistent drawing style do the work.

#### Cited Findings
- Hargreaves: one character, one dominant trait, bright bold marker drawing; over 90 characters sustained on this basis; characters cross into each other's books — [Wikipedia, Mr. Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Men); [New Statesman](https://www.newstatesman.com/culture/books/2021/08/fifty-years-of-mr-men-books)
- The first character's design is fully stated in three features: small blue hat, extraordinarily long arms, big smile — [New Statesman](https://www.newstatesman.com/culture/books/2021/08/fifty-years-of-mr-men-books)
- Scarry: animals defined by job and caption; a small set of named regulars; Lowly Worm identified by a green hat, one shoe and an apple car; a tiny hidden character to find — [Literary Hub](https://lithub.com/why-are-so-many-adults-still-obsessed-with-busytown/); [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/chris-ware-richard-scarry)
- Scarry believed children like re-meeting familiar characters — [Literary Hub](https://lithub.com/why-are-so-many-adults-still-obsessed-with-busytown/)
- Scheffler: starts by sketching the main character; resolved the title creature through rounds of softening and by adding features the text does not mention; kept his animals unclothed at the author's request; plants a cameo of his best-known creature in later books — [Royal Mint interview](https://www.royalmint.com/stories/commemorate/axel-scheffler-reflects-on-the-gruffalos-child/); [Picturebook Makers](https://blog.picturebookmakers.com/post/axel-scheffler); [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)
- Donaldson builds the monster's identity as a spoken list of concrete features repeated with one adjective, and chose its name for sound (harsh opening consonants, echo of "gruff") and for rhyme — [Wikipedia, The Gruffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gruffalo)

#### Inferences
- Five levers appear across the three, in different mixes:
  1. Name that does work: the name is the trait (Hargreaves), the name is a sound that rhymes and suggests character (Donaldson), or the name is a plain job title (Scarry).
  2. Silhouette: one geometric body and one colour (Hargreaves); one unmistakable prop or vehicle (Scarry); species plus a consistent house style of eyes and posture (Scheffler).
  3. One trait or one want: a fixed trait (Hargreaves), a job (Scarry), a goal for this story only (Donaldson: get through the wood, earn the star).
  4. A repeated line: the refrain or catchphrase belongs to the character and is how a pre-reader "says" them (Donaldson strongest; Hargreaves through the narrator's stock phrases; Scarry hardly at all).
  5. A fixed place in the world: a house, a job, a vehicle, a spot on the map, so the child knows where to find them.
- Cast size per book differs sharply: Donaldson typically uses a hero plus three or four others; Hargreaves one lead plus one or two visitors; Scarry dozens at once but only a few with names. A large series cast is therefore usually introduced a few at a time, with crowd scenes reserved for a "busy" format.
- For an original animal cast, the transferable (non-copying) principles are: each character recognisable as a black silhouette; one prop or colour never shared; a name with a distinct sound and syllable count; a like or skill in place of a fault; a line or sound the child can say; and one small recurring thing hidden in every book.

#### Gaps
- No interview was found in which any of the three creators sets out explicit rules for cast design; the levers above are inferred from the work and the cited descriptions.
- Catchphrases for individual Scarry or Hargreaves characters were not sourced.

### 1. Bluey: episode structure, how an episode grows from a game, the parents, play, emotional truth, dual audience, Brumm's process, Brisbane

#### Takeaway
A Bluey episode is a seven-minute single-plot short film that usually starts from a real game or family conflict Joe Brumm noted down, is driven by the emotions that arise inside the game (jealousy, regret), has no antagonist and no curriculum, and lets any lesson emerge from the play itself. The parents are equal partners who join the game and take their roles seriously while being visibly reluctant or tired, and the setting is a specific, recognisable Brisbane.

#### Cited Findings

Format and length
- Running time is 7 minutes; the show was ordered as 52 seven-minute episodes. Exceptions: "The Sign" (Series 3) runs 28 minutes; "Minisodes" run one to three minutes. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- A conflicting figure: one source says the content of each episode is roughly seven minutes and most episodes are nine minutes with credits (via search summary, not read in full). — [The Mary Sue](https://themarysue.com/how-long-are-bluey-episodes-answered)
- Brumm has said he aims to stick to a three-act structure or story circle so that it feels as though a character has been somewhere, and that the writers "rarely have B stories and just condense and condense" until the episode reaches length (via search summary; the summary did not make clear which page carried the quote, most likely the Ragan article). — [Ragan: Lessons on life, writing and storytelling from the creator of Bluey](https://ragan.com/?p=288582)
- Ragan's own summary line says Brumm "shares guidance on finding the concise core of a story". — [Ragan](https://ragan.com/?p=288582)

How an episode grows from a game
- Brumm writes most scripts. His process "sometimes begins with making notes about his family's experiences", including the games his children play and the conflicts between them. He described each episode as "a chance to make a short film". — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Brumm said he started writing down the games his children played, which often devolved into "'Monty Python' bizarre scenarios"; the "Ticklecrabs" game was originally called Pinchy, after a crab he pretended would sneak home from the beach with him (via search summary; NPR page timed out). — [NPR: The creator of Bluey writes for both kids and grown-ups](https://www.npr.org/2022/11/29/1139765338/the-creator-of-bluey-writes-for-both-kids-and-grown-ups)
- Brumm: the best episodes have "wild premises that follow rigid logic", and "Each episode has a number of components to make it good … It's not just about coming up with a funny game." (via search summary; exact page not confirmed, results pointed to the Deadline interview syndicated on Yahoo and to World Screen). — [Deadline via Yahoo](https://malaysia.news.yahoo.com/bluey-creator-joe-brumm-making-131830300.html); [World Screen](https://worldscreen.com/tvkids/?p=22314)
- Bluey and Bingo play during ordinary activities such as a supermarket trip. Daley Pearson (Ludo co-founder, named "Pearson" in the article) said the emotions in play, such as jealousy and regret, drive the conflicts, and that there is no antagonist. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Writer Tim Bain's Bluey episode came from his own memory of riding on his dad's shoulders; he credits Bluey with blending relatable comedy with a discovery that resolves a character's problem. — [Skwigly: Tim Bain Q&A](https://www.skwigly.co.uk/tim-bain-writing-childrens-kids-animation/)

Production process
- "It starts with me writing the episodes. Once we have those, we're all under one roof in the middle of Brisbane at Ludo Studio. There are 50 or 60 of us." (Brumm, via search summary). — [World Screen](https://worldscreen.com/tvkids/?p=22314)
- Script writing takes up to two months after the idea; storyboard artists make 500 to 800 drawings over three weeks; a black-and-white animatic is cut with separately recorded dialogue; animation, backgrounds, design and layout take four weeks; a complete episode takes three to four months and about fifteen are in development at once. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- The whole team watches a near-finished episode on Fridays; these became test screenings with families and friends. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Producer Sam Moor said few writers besides Brumm work on the series, mostly animators already on the team. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Brumm conceived the idea while working on Charlie and Lola, considered Peppa Pig as a model, and cited Peanuts and The Simpsons. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))

Child development and play
- Brumm said watching his two daughters play was "as natural to them as breathing". He wanted to show that unstructured, self-directed play shapes children, and consulted research on socio-dramatic play by Sara Smilansky and Vivian Paley. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- After his daughter struggled with formal schooling he left out literacy and numeracy and focused on life skills; he wanted to depict his experience as a parent and not explicitly teach. "There's no counting in Bluey, there's no learning this or that ... just show 'em playing." (Brumm, 2019). — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Developmental researchers (Molly Scott, Temple; Aria Gastón-Panthaki, Harvard; Douglas Piper, Georgetown) say the show exemplifies the finding that children learn through play, and that pretend play lets children process fears and feelings. Their examples: "Copycat" (Bluey re-enacts finding an injured bird and insists the game end in bad news; Chilli, playing the vet, follows her direction), "Early Baby" (Indy and classmates play out a sibling in neonatal intensive care), "Rain" (Chilli helps build the dam instead of stopping it), "Flatpack" (packaging becomes an imaginary world), "Wagon Ride" (Bandit invents a hand-on-arm signal so Bluey can get his attention without interrupting), "Grannies" and "Phones" (grandparents drawn into play, including by video call). — [The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/bluey-teaches-children-and-parents-alike-about-how-play-supports-creativity/)

No stated moral
- Lessons (following rules, the influence of technology and money) emerge through gameplay; the article describes no stated moral. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))

The parents
- Both parents guide and join in play and validate the children's emotions; critics describe them as equal partners. Bandit is an archaeologist who does housework and plays imaginative games; USA Today's Jennifer McClellan called him "sarcastic, sympathetic and silly" and Chilli the "voice of reason". Chilli works part-time in airport security, and flashbacks show her struggles as a new mother. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Bandit takes his game roles seriously despite often seeming reluctant; his attempts to teach life lessons sometimes strain things with his daughters. Chilli is level-headed, balances the chaos, and joins in when she wants. — [Wikipedia: List of Bluey characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_characters)
- Brumm confirmed Bandit is an extension of himself (via search summary). — [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/bluey-gold-pen-game-inspiration-joe-brumm-2000696293)

Brisbane on screen
- Set in semi-tropical Queensland with Brisbane as the model: Queenslander houses (high-set, verandas), Queen Street Mall, South Bank, The Big Pelican on the Noosa River; jacaranda and poinciana trees; fruit bats, wallabies, kookaburras, ibises; rugby league and State of Origin. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Regional loyalties are character detail: Lucky supports the Maroons, Mackenzie's parents are All Blacks fans. — [Wikipedia: List of Bluey characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_characters)

#### Inferences
- The working template is: ordinary setting (home, car, shop) + a game with a clear rule + an emotional snag inside the game + the game's own logic supplies the resolution. One plot, no B story, no villain.
- "Wild premise, rigid logic" is in effect a world rule: the game's rules are never broken for convenience, which is what lets a seven-minute story feel fair and complete.
- The dual audience is achieved structurally and not by adult jokes laid on top: the child follows the game, the adult follows the parent inside the game (tired, reluctant, committed anyway). Both are watching the same scene.
- The Friday family screenings are a cheap, repeatable test worth copying for book drafts: read aloud to real children and watch where attention drops.

#### Gaps
- I could not open a primary Brumm interview in full, so quotes on structure are second-hand. The "rules" below are from background knowledge and are unverified in this session: Brumm has described a rule that episodes are told from the children's point of view and that games must be ones children could really play; the writers' room is small and Brumm wrote almost every episode of Series 1 to 3; Brumm announced in late 2024 that he was stepping back from writing the series to work on the feature film. Check before relying on these.
- No source found tying the seven-minute length to research; it appears to be a broadcaster slot length.
- No script page count found.

### 2. Bluey's characters: how the cast is kept distinct

#### Takeaway
Each character is fixed by a small set of markers: an exact age, a breed (which doubles as surname, silhouette and colour), one or two temperament words, a family circumstance, and one recurring behaviour or want. Speech differences are used sparingly but precisely (Muffin's L/R sounds, Socks barking, Jean-Luc's French, Pom Pom's squeak).

Breed and colour as identity
- The family is "modelled after real-life blue and red Australian cattle dogs or heelers"; each supporting character is a different dog breed (Coco a pink Poodle, Chloe a Dalmatian). Bluey and Bingo are girls with the "rough and tumble" traits of real puppies. Brumm based Bluey on his childhood Blue Heeler. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- More than 50 dog breeds appear in the show (via search summary of consumer press; not an official count). — [Romper](https://www.romper.com/entertainment/what-kind-of-dog-is-bluey)
- Colour palette described by Sam Moor as "a vibrant pastel". — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))

#### Inferences
- Breed supplies three things at once: a one-glance silhouette and colour, a surname, and a temperament hint (herding Border Collie, tireless Kelpie, tidy Beagle). For a non-animal cast the equivalent is a signature colour, shape and one object.
- Blue-for-a-girl and orange-for-a-girl in the lead family means colour marks the individual and the family line and not gender.
- Each friend is given a home circumstance that generates stories without being a "topic" (army dad, new baby, diet, moved country, divorce, shy).
- Ages are spaced: 1, 3, 4, 6. Each age is a different developmental stage, so each child wants and can do different things in the same game. That spacing does a lot of the characterisation work.
- Flaws are age-true and not moral failings (Muffin's tantrums are what three looks like).

### 3. Bluey books: formats, how an episode becomes a book, counts, publisher, sales

#### Takeaway
Penguin Random House Australia has published Bluey books since November 2019 and reports over 10 million sold across 100 titles in Australia and New Zealand. Episode adaptations come mainly as 24-page board books (often with a novelty such as flaps or glow-in-the-dark) and 32-page hardback picture books that keep the episode's title; activity formats run alongside.

#### Cited Findings
- April 2019: BBC Studios partnered with Penguin Random House Australia. First three titles on 5 November 2019: The Beach, Fruit Bat, and the sticker book Time to Play!. All three topped the Australian weekly charts that month and sold 350,000 combined by January 2020. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))

### 4. Other properties: Peppa Pig, Hey Duggee, Daniel Tiger, Maisy, Sarah & Duck

Daniel Tiger is the only one built on an explicit curriculum, delivered as a repeated sung strategy.

### 5. Series bibles and character profiles for preschool

#### Takeaway
Industry guidance agrees on four parts: world, characters, stories, format. Character entries cover background, goals, emotional tone, look, speech and above all relationships. I found no published preschool-specific template and no source that uses the term "story engine"; the nearest documented equivalents are the "episode structure" page and the "rules of the world" page.

#### Cited Findings
- Jeffrey Scott (700+ animation scripts) names four vital elements: the World (setting, key locations, recurring objects), the Characters, the Stories, the Format. Characters: "backgrounds, their goals and motivations, their emotional tone, their look and dress", plus relationships. Stories: "a half dozen to an entire season's worth of story ideas", each a short beginning, middle and end showing how the elements fit. Format: what the show is about, theme, genre, episode length, style, role of music, target age. For Muppet Babies the nursery's furniture and toys were chosen because they let the characters play out stories, and "The theme of the show was imagination." — [AnimationXpress: The four vital elements of an animated series bible](https://www.animationxpress.com/latest-news/guest-column-htwfa-31-the-four-vital-elements-of-an-animated-series-bible/)
- A pitch bible typically has cover with title and key image, logline of one or two sentences, synopsis, characters (only those in most episodes), world, storylines and episode structure, at about 20 pages and no more than 40. A writers' bible adds character relationships, location detail, detailed episode structure and curriculum (via search summary). — [Prime the Animation: Animated TV series bibles (PDF)](https://primetheanimation.upv.es/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/AnimatedTVSeriesBibles_PrimetheAnimation2020.pdf)
- One creator's order: logline, concept page, per-episode structure page, rules of the world; in animation anything goes as long as the rules stay consistent. State target age on the cover, splitting preschool into 1 to 3 and 3 to 5; put children front and centre with adults in support (via search summary). — [Pick Up Your Pen: Developing a kids' animation series, part two](https://pickupyourpen.com/developing-a-kids-animated-tv-series-part-2/)
- Character bibles should include speech patterns and vocabulary, contradictions, and how the character responds under pressure (via search summary; a studio blog, lower authority). — [Whizzy Studios](https://www.whizzystudios.com/post/how-to-write-a-character-bible-for-kids-animation)
- Tim Bain (writer on Bluey and other preschool shows): "Write a bible, look at other bibles if you can"; preschool writing means "focusing on a character's inner most emotions" with "a lot of heart and empathy"; children swing "between different emotions from one second to the next"; adults "definitely take a back seat"; stories follow children's own goals such as "seeking fun or making friendships"; read dialogue aloud. — [Skwigly](https://www.skwigly.co.uk/tim-bain-writing-childrens-kids-animation/)
- General TV guidance: recurring characters should have their own wants and problems (via search summary). — [Final Draft](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/10-steps-to-developing-your-tv-show-bible)
- Kidscreen Summit has run a mentor class titled "Building bibles that work", but the listing gives no content. — [Kidscreen Summit](https://summit.kidscreen.com/2018/sessions/68752/soldoutmentorclass)
- Daniel Tiger shows what a curriculum statement looks like in practice: a named list of learning goals, one theme per story, and one repeatable strategy. — [Fred Rogers Productions](https://www.fredrogers.org/production/daniel-tigers-neighborhood/)
- Bluey shows the opposite values statement: no numeracy or literacy, life skills only, "just show 'em playing". — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))

#### Inferences
- A workable profile template, assembled from the sources above and from how Bluey's cast is actually differentiated: name; exact age; one-line essence; look (colour, shape, signature object); want; fear or soft spot; age-true flaw; recurring behaviour; how they speak (vocabulary size, catchphrase, sound); home and family circumstance; relationship to each other regular; how they behave under pressure; what they never do. The "never does" line and the "fear" line are common practice in my experience but were not found stated in a source here.
- "Story engine" can be written as one sentence that generates episodes, for example Hey Duggee's "the Squirrels meet something new and earn the badge for it" or Bluey's "an ordinary family moment becomes a game, and the game teaches someone something". A bible should then list ten or more springboards that prove the engine runs.
- "World rules" for preschool usually cover: whose point of view; whether adults can solve the problem (normally not); whether imagination is shown as real on the page; what never appears (villains, screens, peril); how every story ends.
- A values or curriculum statement should say what the series is for and also what it deliberately does not teach.

#### Gaps
- No published preschool bible (Bluey, Peppa, Duggee) was found online; these are not public. The Prime the Animation PDF was not opened directly.
- No Kidscreen article setting out profile contents was found. The phrase "story engine" did not appear in any source retrieved.
- No source gave a stated list of "what a character never does".

### 6. Writing a baby and a toddler who barely speak; giving parents and grandparents a real place

#### Takeaway
The successful examples give the pre-verbal child a physical repertoire, one sound or word, and a clear want, and let an older sibling, parent or narrator interpret. Adults earn their place by joining the child's game on the child's terms and by having small visible feelings of their own, while the child's goal drives the story.

#### Cited Findings
- Socks (age one, later two) begins by barking and walking on all fours, chews things, and over the series learns to walk and talk and then to form sentences; her growth is visible across seasons. — [Wikipedia: List of Bluey characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_characters); [Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/bluey-socks-dog-behavior-explained/)
- Muffin at three is written through volume, tantrums, selfishness and a speech pattern (trouble with L and R), with a good heart underneath. — [Wikipedia: List of Bluey characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_characters)
- Jean-Luc and Bluey share no language and "communicate through play". — [Wikipedia: List of Bluey characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_characters)
- Peppa Pig's narrator says "Oh, dear" when George cries, supplying the comment the toddler cannot. — [Wikipedia: Peppa Pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peppa_Pig)
- Sarah & Duck's narrator converses with a child and a non-speaking duck, asking questions and making suggestions. — [Wikipedia: Sarah & Duck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_%26_Duck)
- Babies as story circumstance for older children: Chloe's baby brother, Indy's sister born early (played out by the children in "Early Baby"), Lila's brother who cannot talk yet. — [Wikipedia: List of Bluey characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_characters); [The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/bluey-teaches-children-and-parents-alike-about-how-play-supports-creativity/)
- Parents: both join the play and validate feelings; Bandit commits to his role while seeming reluctant; Chilli follows Bluey's direction inside the game in "Copycat" even though hesitant; she helps build the dam in "Rain" and does not stop it. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series)); [The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/bluey-teaches-children-and-parents-alike-about-how-play-supports-creativity/)
- Parents are shown as learners too: Stripe is inexperienced; Stripe and Trixie argue about parenting and find a middle ground; Chilli's early struggles as a mother appear in flashback. — [Wikipedia: List of Bluey characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_characters)
- Grandparents are taught by the children: Nana learns a dance over video call in "Grannies"; Grandad learns about apps with cardboard phones in "Phones" and invents his own. Researchers note grandparent and grandchild bonds benefit both and that video chat supports them. Nana is indulgent with grandchildren; Mort is ex-Army and energetic despite age. — [The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/bluey-teaches-children-and-parents-alike-about-how-play-supports-creativity/); [Wikipedia: List of Bluey characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_characters)
- Industry rule of thumb: adults "definitely take a back seat" and children are front and centre. — [Skwigly](https://www.skwigly.co.uk/tim-bain-writing-childrens-kids-animation/); [Pick Up Your Pen](https://pickupyourpen.com/developing-a-kids-animated-tv-series-part-2/)
- Daniel Tiger takes the other route: adults model the strategy for the child. — [HuffPost](https://www.huffingtonpost.ca/entry/fred-rogers-daniel-tigers-neighborhood_us_5b1fe6cee4b09d7a3d777355)

#### Inferences
- Baby profile: list what the baby can do at this exact age (grab, crawl, point, drop, laugh), one signature sound, one thing she always wants (her sister's things, a particular object), and who interprets her. The baby works best as a source of interruptions and accidents that redirect the toddler's game, and as someone the toddler has feelings about.
- Toddler lead profile: a handful of real words and phrases used consistently, big fast-changing feelings, a strong will, and action carried by the pictures. A narrator or the book's text voice speaks around her and not for her.
- Letting the non-verbal character develop over the series (Socks) rewards repeat readers and matches the audience's own growth.
- Two adult models to choose between or mix: the Bluey parent who is a player inside the game with their own small arc, and the Daniel Tiger parent who supplies a strategy. In both, the adult does not solve the problem for the child. Parents can be given one honest feeling per story (tired, distracted, wanting to finish a cup of tea) without taking the plot.
- Grandparents work well as the ones who are taught by the child, who have more time and looser rules than parents, and who carry a different pace and a different house.

- No craft source specifically on writing pre-verbal characters in preschool animation or picture books was found; this section rests on examples and inference.
- George Pig's single-word speech and Baby Margaret in Daniel Tiger were not sourced in this session.
- No maker's statement found on why Socks was written as non-verbal at first.

# Part 6. Notes: Stories on a screen, and Australian books

## Digital picture books and story apps for under-fives, and the Australian picture-book tradition for the very young

Reading notes on labels used below: **[Research]** = peer-reviewed study or meta-analysis; **[Official]** = health body or platform rule; **[Maker]** = a publisher's or developer's own opinion or practice; **[Secondary]** = a summary by a third party, original not read. Anything listed under "Gaps" as "background knowledge, unverified" comes from the researcher's memory and was not confirmed against a source in this session.

### 1. Screen guidance for under-fives (WHO, AAP, Australian Government, Raising Children Network)

#### Takeaway
All four bodies agree: no sedentary screen time for babies (WHO under 1; Australia under 2; AAP under 18 months except video chat), then at most one hour a day up to age 5, with "less is better". All of them frame shared reading and storytelling with a caregiver as the preferred sedentary activity, and the AAP says an e-book should be used the way a print book is: adult and child together, with minimal bells and whistles, and not at bedtime.

#### Cited Findings
- [Official] WHO (April 2019): screen time is not recommended for children under 1; for 2-year-olds sedentary screen time should be no more than 1 hour, less is better; same limit for ages 3 to 4. — [WHO news release](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more)
- [Official] WHO: when sedentary, "engaging in reading and storytelling with a caregiver is encouraged"; quality sedentary time is interactive, non-screen activities with a caregiver such as reading, storytelling, singing and puzzles. Children should not be restrained (pram, high chair) more than 1 hour at a time. — [WHO news release](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more)
- [Official] Australian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for the Early Years (birth to 5): under 2, sedentary screen time is not recommended; age 2, no more than 1 hour, less is better; ages 3 to 5, no more than 1 hour a day, less is better. When sedentary, reading and storytelling with a caregiver is encouraged. — [Australian Government Department of Health fact sheet](https://www.health.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/2021/05/24-hour-movement-guidelines-birth-to-5-years-fact-sheet.pdf); [health.gov.au overview](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians)
- [Secondary] The Australian guidelines also advise avoiding screens before sleep and keeping screens out of the bedroom. (From a search summary of the government brochure and childcare-sector restatements; exact wording not read in the primary.) — [Department of Health brochure](https://www.health.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/2021/05/24-hour-movement-guidelines-birth-to-5-years-brochure.docx)
- [Secondary] AAP 2016 policy ("Media and Young Minds"): under 18 months, discourage screen media other than video-chatting; 18 to 24 months, parents who want to introduce media should choose high-quality programming and watch it with the child to help them understand. — [Education Week report on the 2016 AAP policy](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/pediatricians-set-new-guidelines-on-electronic-media-use-by-young-children/2016/10)
- [Secondary] AAP-based guidance for ages 2 to 5: under one hour a day of good-quality programming or apps. — [KidsHealth](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/screentime-preschool.html)
- [Official] AAP early literacy toolkit on e-books for ages 0 to 5: with print books parents and children "are chattier, snuggle in together more, and their conversations are richer than with an electronic book"; e-books for young children "are associated with lower comprehension and attention to content"; e-books "that contain bells and whistles can distract from the actual book content" or take over the adult's role; children learn best from a "simple and streamlined design that focuses on the content"; e-books "might be best avoided at bedtime or before naptime"; use screen reading as "a jumping off point for parent-child interactions". — [AAP Early Literacy Toolkit: Electronic Books and Audio Books for Children 0-5](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/electronic-books-and-audio-books-for-children-0-5-years-of-age/)
- [Official] The same AAP page warns that with interactive features families can end up negotiating "who gets to do them" instead of talking about the story, and that some children find it harder to transition away from a tablet. — [AAP Early Literacy Toolkit](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/electronic-books-and-audio-books-for-children-0-5-years-of-age/)
- [Secondary] Raising Children Network (Australian parenting site): national and international guidelines recommend no screen time under 2 other than video-chatting; for ages 2 to 5, screen use can be part of a healthy lifestyle when balanced with other activities; good-quality apps can support development (for example by encouraging turn-taking); screen use before bed can affect how quickly and how well a child sleeps. (Search-result fragments only; the site returned HTTP 403 to direct fetches.) — [Raising Children Network: healthy screen time 2-5 years](https://raisingchildren.net.au/preschoolers/play-learning/screen-time-healthy-screen-use/healthy-screen-time-2-5-years); [Raising Children Network: healthy screen time 0-2 years](https://raisingchildren.net.au/newborns/play-learning/media-technology/healthy-screen-time-0-2-years)
- [Secondary] Child Trends: children as young as 18 months may learn from digital media when co-viewing or co-playing with an engaged adult; preschoolers learn best from well-designed e-books with limited distracting features such as games and sounds. — [Child Trends](https://childtrends.org/publications/5-ways-screen-time-can-benefit-children-and-families)

#### Inferences
- The guidelines limit "sedentary screen time"; none of them treats a parent reading a story on a screen with a child as equivalent to solo video watching, but none exempts it either. A story app is on safest ground when it is short, designed for an adult and child together, calm, and not pitched at bedtime (or has a deliberately low-stimulation, dim, audio-light bedtime mode; that is an inference, not a guideline).
- For under-2s the official position in Australia is no screen time. A product that says "from birth" or "0+" will sit against that guidance; positioning for 2 to 5 with the adult as reader for younger siblings is the defensible framing.
- A session-length design target of well under one hour total, with natural stopping points (the end of a book), aligns with all four bodies.

#### Gaps
- The full text of the AAP 2016 policy statement was not read; its wording is reported via secondary sources. The AAP is understood to have been moving away from fixed time limits toward a quality-and-context framing in more recent material; this was not verified.
- Raising Children Network pages could not be fetched (403). Its specific advice on e-books and on co-use was not confirmed beyond search fragments.

### 2. Research on e-books and story apps: animation, sound, hotspots, games; print versus digital

#### Takeaway
The consistent research finding is that features which carry the story (animation, music and sound effects that match the narration) help comprehension and vocabulary, while features that sit beside the story (hotspots, games, embedded dictionaries, unrelated sounds) hurt comprehension and displace story talk. Print still wins on parent-child talk with toddlers, and a plain digitised book can underperform print; but a digital book with story-congruent enhancements can outperform print, and nothing beats an adult mediating.

#### Cited Findings
- [Research] Takacs, Swart & Bus (2015), meta-analysis, Review of Educational Research 85(4): multimedia features (animated pictures, music, sound effects) were beneficial for story comprehension and vocabulary; interactive features (hotspots, games, dictionaries) were distracting. For children from less stimulating home environments, multimedia helped most and interactive features were detrimental. — [Takacs, Swart & Bus 2015 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647204)
- [Research] Takacs, Swart & Bus (2014), Frontiers in Psychology: multimedia stories' illustrations, background music and sound effects can provide scaffolding for story comprehension and word learning similar to an adult's. — [Takacs, Swart & Bus 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01366/pdf)
- [Research] Furenes, Kucirkova & Bus (2021), meta-analysis of 39 studies (1,812 children aged 1 to 8), Review of Educational Research 91(4): when paper and digital differed only in digitisation, comprehension was lower on screen; adult mediation during print reading was more effective than digital enhancements used by a child alone; when enhancements were story-congruent, digital books outperformed paper; an embedded dictionary had no or a negative effect on comprehension but helped vocabulary. — [Furenes, Kucirkova & Bus 2021 (Open University repository)](https://oro.open.ac.uk/75652); [AERA summary](https://www.aera.net/tabid/19370/mid/50405/default.aspx)
- [Research] Munzer et al. (2019), Pediatrics: 37 parent-toddler pairs read enhanced e-books (sound, animation), basic e-books and print. With print, parents produced more dialogic talk and toddlers more book-related talk; collaboration scores were higher. With tablets, parents made more device-focused statements (how to swipe, don't press that). The difference held for basic e-books too, suggesting the tablet itself, and not only the enhancements, changes the interaction. Caveats: small sample, lab setting, five-minute readings. — [Reach Out and Read summary of Munzer et al.](https://reachoutandread.org/article/differences-in-parent-toddler-interactions-with-electronic-versus-print-books/); [University of Michigan release](https://michigan.it.umich.edu/news/2019/03/28/reading-to-your-toddler-print-books-are-better-than-digital-ones); [AAP news release](https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/pediatrics2/2019/parents-and-children-interact-with-print-and-electronic-books-differrently/)
- [Research, small and non-peer-reviewed QuickReport] Joan Ganz Cooney Center (2012): 32 parent-child pairs, children aged 3 to 6. Print and basic e-books produced similar content-related talk; with the enhanced e-book, pairs engaged less with story content, although children showed more physical engagement. — [Cooney Center QuickReport: Print Books vs. E-books](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/?p=6824)
- [Critique] Librarians' commentary on that QuickReport: sample small and demographically narrow; the enhanced book was built for solo use; the only statistically significant finding was less labelling/pointing with the enhanced e-book; children were actually more distracted with print than with the basic e-book (4.1 versus 2.3 distracted moments). — [American Libraries](https://americanlibrariesmagazine.org/blogs/e-content/obvious-ebook-research-is-obvious-and-oblivious/)
- [Research framework] Hirsh-Pasek, Zosh et al. (2015), Psychological Science in the Public Interest: apps support learning when they promote active ("minds-on"), engaged (not distracted), meaningful, and socially interactive experience, in the service of a learning goal. Physical action alone (tapping, swiping) does not count as active involvement. — [Association for Psychological Science](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/educational-apps.html); [New America summary](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/new-framework-identify-truly-educational-apps/)
- [Research] Word highlighting / read-along: in a pre-K eye-tracking study (Arslan-Ari & Ari 2021), about 82% of fixations were on pictures and about 16% on text; roughly half of the text fixations coincided with the narration-synced cue; only two children improved on word recognition (single session, small sample). — [Journal of Research on Technology in Education](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15391523.2021.1938763)
- [Research] A 2015 Reading and Writing eye-tracking study found pointing to and highlighting text increased time and fixations on print, but preschoolers' fixations did not track the narration word by word. — [Reading and Writing 2015](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/10.1007/s11145-015-9571-2)
- [Research] Takacs & Bus (2016): hypothesis and evidence that motion guides attention to the illustration details the text is describing, which may explain better comprehension from animated than static books. — [Leiden University repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2872121/view)
- [Research] Kucirkova: digital personalisation can both strengthen and undermine agency for child and adult; her framework stresses agency plus reciprocity (the shared, social side of reading), warning against "overly child-centred" design that drops the adult. — [London Review of Education article](https://journals.uclpress.co.uk/lre/article/id/352); [Kucirkova, How and Why to Read and Create Children's Digital Books (open access)](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/26110/1/How-and-Why-to-Read-and-Create-Children%27s-Digital-Books.pdf)
- [Secondary] University of Stavanger teacher resource on the 2021 meta-analysis: distracting digital books and enhancements not aligned with the storyline impede comprehension. — [University of Stavanger resource](https://training.uis.no/sites/default/files/2022-03/Childrens%20digital%20books%20-%20utskriftsvennlig%20versjon%2020210921.pdf)
- [Secondary] A 2022 commentary recommended that software designers limit extraneous enhancements and that clinicians keep promoting co-viewing. — [C.S. Mott Children's Hospital research highlights](https://issuu.com/umhs/docs/csmott_research_report-2022/21)

#### Inferences
- A working rule from the evidence: **an enhancement is safe if removing it would make the story harder to follow, and suspect if the story reads the same without it.** Motion that shows what the sentence says (the wombat digs as the text says "dig") helps; a tappable butterfly that giggles does not.
- A digital lift-the-flap is an interactive feature, so it lands on the risky side of the Takacs/Bus line unless the flap *is* the story beat (the reveal answers the question the text just asked, as in "Where is...?" books). One flap per spread, on the narrative path, with nothing else tappable, is the configuration most consistent with the research. No study of digital flaps specifically was found.
- Munzer's "device talk" finding implies the biggest win is making the mechanics invisible: one gesture, no settings visible in the story, no accidental exits, so the adult talks about the story and not the tablet.
- Word highlighting is low-cost and may nudge attention to print in 4- to 5-year-olds, but evidence for learning from it under 5 is weak; it should be optional and visually quiet, and it is irrelevant for under-3s.
- Games, quizzes and embedded dictionaries should be kept out of the story path (if present at all, after the last page).

#### Gaps
- No study specific to Australian children or to digital lift-the-flap mechanics was found.
- Parish-Morris et al.'s work on electronic console books and dialogic reading was not retrieved.
- Tiffany Munzer's later studies (for example on tablet nursery-rhyme apps and on toddlers' responses to parents during tablet reading) were seen only as headlines, not read.
- Most studies are of children aged 3 to 6; evidence for under-2s on story apps is thin.
- Many studies predate current app design; effect sizes were not extracted.

### 3. Good practice from makers of story apps and digital picture books

#### Takeaway
Platform rules (Apple) are firm: no links out, no purchases and no distractions except behind a parental gate; no third-party ads or analytics in practice; a privacy policy. Makers and practitioner research (Sesame Workshop, Nosy Crow, Sago Mini, Google's kids guidance, Nielsen Norman Group) converge on: tap as the primary gesture, big targets, nothing important near the bottom edge, landscape on tablets, spoken and visual cues in place of written instructions, both narrated and un-narrated reading modes, and interactivity that serves the story. These are opinions and conventions, not tested findings, except where noted.

#### Cited Findings
- [Official] Apple App Store Review Guideline 1.3 (Kids Category): apps "must not include links out of the app, purchasing opportunities, or other distractions to kids unless reserved for a designated area behind a parental gate"; may not send personally identifiable or device information to third parties; "should not include third-party analytics or third-party advertising" (limited exceptions: analytics that collect no IDFA or identifiable information; contextual ads only from services with documented Kids policies and human review of creatives). Once in the Kids Category, an app must keep meeting the rules in later updates even if the category is deselected. — [Apple App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- [Official] Apple Guideline 5.1.4: apps in the Kids Category must include a privacy policy and comply with children's privacy laws (COPPA, GDPR and others); a parental gate is not the same as verifiable parental consent to collect data; terms such as "For Kids" in metadata are reserved for the Kids Category (2.3.8). — [Apple App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)
- [Maker/practitioner research] Sesame Workshop best practices for preschool tablet design: tapping is the most intuitive gesture for preschoolers; pinching and flicking are often difficult; preschoolers tend to hold tablets in landscape; keep hotspots and icons away from the bottom edge because children rest their wrists there and trigger unintended actions; pair spoken dialogue with visual reinforcement because preschoolers usually cannot read; in e-books offer modes with and without narration; let parents turn off distracting hotspots; design for "joint media engagement" and include parent tips. — [Joan Ganz Cooney Center: Best Practices: Designing Touch Tablet Experiences for Preschoolers](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/publication/best-practices-designing-touch-tablet-experiences-for-preschoolers/)
- [Secondary] The Sesame document ranks tap, draw/move finger, swipe, drag and slide as the most intuitive gestures, and pinch, tilt/shake, multi-touch, flick/fling and double tap as the least. (Reported via a paper citing it.) — [CORE-hosted paper citing Sesame Workshop](https://core.ac.uk/download/288380419.pdf)
- [Practitioner research] Nielsen Norman Group: touch targets for young children should be at least 2 cm by 2 cm (versus 1 cm for adults); the younger the child, the bigger the target; under-5s struggle with drag-and-drop, so offer tap-then-tap as an alternative and more than one way to complete an action. — [NN/g: Children's UX and physical development](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/children-ux-physical-development/); [NN/g: Touch target size](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/touch-target-size/)
- [Official/platform guidance] Google's building-for-kids guidance: use draw, swipe and drag; avoid multi-finger, double-tap and pinch gestures for young audiences. — [Google: Designing engaging apps](https://developers.google.com/building-for-kids/designing-engaging-apps)
- [Maker] Nosy Crow (UK publisher of fairy-tale story apps): its principle is "reading experiences and not just games"; apps are designed to work for a child alone or shared with an adult, with "Read and play" and "Read by myself" modes; "It's not about adding bells and whistles to existing books but creating new kinds of reading experience using multimedia and interactivity"; the team's recurring question is "Who's it for?"; the focus is reading for pleasure and not a curriculum. Writing an interactive app is "not the same as writing a picture book". — [Nosy Crow blog: Appy days](https://nosycrow.com/blog/appy-days); [Cooney Center podcast transcript with Nosy Crow](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/2018/02/22/podcast-transcript-the-app-fairy-talks-to-nosy-crow/); [Publishing Perspectives](https://publishingperspectives.com/2012/08/how-is-writing-a-childrens-book-different-than-writing-an-app/); [Horn Book: What Makes a Good Picture Book App?](https://www.hbook.com/story/what-makes-a-good-picture-book-app)
- [Maker] Sago Mini describes itself as making thoughtfully designed apps for children five and under; its apps carry no text or voiceover, in line with an open-play, no-goal philosophy. — [Cooney Center Q&A with Sago Sago's Jason Krogh](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/2014/11/14/qa-with-sago-sagos-jason-krogh/); [Kidscreen](https://kidscreen.com/2015/04/09/sago-sago-dishes-on-developing-for-the-preschool-market)
- [Secondary, low authority] Third-party reviewers describe Sago Mini World as calm, with no ads and no in-app shop inside a subscription, and Pok Pok as "low-arousal"; Toca Boca's approach is described as setting out elements and letting children play. — [Screenwise review of Sago Mini World](https://screenwiseapp.com/media/sago-mini-world-game); [InVision: Building apps for children](https://invisionapp.com/inside-design/building-apps-for-children)
- [Official, AAP] Simple, streamlined design focused on content is what children learn from best; avoid "pervasive distractions, animation, and noise". — [AAP Early Literacy Toolkit](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/electronic-books-and-audio-books-for-children-0-5-years-of-age/)

#### Inferences
Checklist assembled from the above (maker convention unless marked):
- **Page turns:** one obvious way forward (tap an edge arrow or swipe; both accepted). Under-3s tap more reliably than they swipe. Do not auto-advance in shared-reading mode, because the adult sets the pace.
- **Tap targets:** 2 cm square minimum (about 75 to 80 points on an iPhone is a reasonable translation; the conversion is an inference), generous spacing, forgiving hit areas larger than the art.
- **Bottom edge and corners:** nothing tappable where a wrist or a gripping thumb rests; the exit/home control top-corner, small, and requiring a hold or a parental gate.
- **Modes:** "Read to me" (narration on), "Read it myself / read together" (narration off, the adult is the voice). Research supports the adult-as-reader mode as the default for under-3s.
- **Digital flaps:** tap to open (not drag), the flap stays open until the page turns or re-closes on tap, one meaningful flap per spread, and the story still makes sense if the flap is never touched: no dead ends.
- **No dead ends, no fail states:** every screen is completable by tapping; nothing to win or lose (Sago Mini/Pok Pok style).
- **Handing the device over:** all purchases, links, settings and account screens behind a parental gate (Apple requires this); recommend Guided Access in parent tips.
- **No ads, no third-party analytics, a privacy policy:** effectively mandatory in Apple's Kids Category.
- **Accessibility:** narration as the non-reader's access route; captions/text for deaf or hard-of-hearing parents and children; sound never the only carrier of a story beat; reduced-motion respected.

#### Gaps
- No primary design statements were found for Toca Boca, Pok Pok, Lovevery, Khan Academy Kids, Me Books, Epic or CBeebies Storytime. Their practices are not documented here beyond low-authority reviews. Background knowledge, unverified: Me Books let families record their own narration over "hotspots" on classic picture-book pages; CBeebies Storytime offered read-to-me and read-myself modes with comprehension prompts for adults; Khan Academy Kids is free with no ads; Pok Pok is subscription-funded with no ads and no language/voice; Lovevery's app is primarily parent-facing.
- Apple's Human Interface Guidelines were not fetched; the 44-point minimum hit target for general apps is from background knowledge, unverified here. Apple's separate "Design for kids"/parental gate pages were not read.
- The Kids Category age bands (5 and under, 6 to 8, 9 to 11) are from background knowledge; one source said "11 and under", another "under 13": unresolved.
- No tested evidence was found on swipe versus tap page turns for under-3s specifically, on digital lift-the-flap interaction, or on the right hit-area size in points.

### 4. Phone versus tablet with a child on a lap: orientation, text size, text per screen, sound

#### Takeaway
There is very little direct research on phone-versus-tablet shared reading for under-fives; the usable facts are that preschoolers hold tablets in landscape, that wrists and thumbs trigger bottom-edge controls, and that the adult-as-reader arrangement produces the best talk. Most of this section is therefore reasoned design guidance, not evidence.

#### Cited Findings
- [Practitioner research] Preschoolers are inclined to hold tablets in landscape; bottom-edge controls get triggered by resting wrists. — [Cooney Center / Sesame Workshop best practices](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/publication/best-practices-designing-touch-tablet-experiences-for-preschoolers/)
- [Research] Tablet reading with toddlers generated more device-management talk and less story talk than print, even for basic e-books. — [Reach Out and Read summary of Munzer et al. 2019](https://reachoutandread.org/article/differences-in-parent-toddler-interactions-with-electronic-versus-print-books/)
- [Official] E-books are best avoided at bedtime or before naps; the AAP flags noise as one of the pervasive distractions. — [AAP Early Literacy Toolkit](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/electronic-books-and-audio-books-for-children-0-5-years-of-age/)
- [Research] Music and sound effects that match the story aid comprehension; unrelated sounds are part of the distracting set. — [Takacs, Swart & Bus 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647204)
- [Research] Preschoolers look at pictures about 82% of the time during narrated e-books. — [Arslan-Ari & Ari 2021](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15391523.2021.1938763)
- [Practitioner research] Offer e-book modes with and without narration. — [Cooney Center / Sesame Workshop best practices](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/publication/best-practices-designing-touch-tablet-experiences-for-preschoolers/)

#### Inferences
- **Orientation:** landscape matches both the double-page spread and how young children hold tablets. On a phone, a landscape spread is small; a phone layout probably needs a re-composed single scene per screen with text in a band, not a shrunken spread. Lock orientation so a wriggling lap does not rotate the page.
- **Who holds it:** on a phone the adult holds and the child taps; hit areas should work for a child reaching across, and adult thumbs on the left/right edges must not turn pages by accident (a deliberate swipe or a tap on a visible arrow, not a tap anywhere on the edge).
- **Text per screen:** the adult is reading at arm's length while a child's head is in the way. One to two short sentences per phone screen (roughly the 10 to 25 words per spread typical of books for under-3s) and large high-contrast type on a calm area of the art. The figure is a convention from board-book practice, not a tested number.
- **Sound:** because the best-evidenced mode is the adult's voice, and phones are often on silent, the story must work fully with sound off. A sensible default: narration off in "read together", respect the hardware mute switch, short story-congruent sound effects only, no looping background music, and a clear spoken-or-visual indication when narration is available. Whether sound should default on or off was not settled by any source.
- **Bedtime:** given official caution, a low-stimulation mode (dark-friendly palette, no sound effects, slow or no animation) is consistent with the guidance, though no body endorses bedtime screens at all.

#### Gaps
- No study comparing phone and tablet for shared reading with under-fives was found.
- No evidence-based minimum type size for adult read-aloud at lap distance on a phone was found.
- No source addressed default sound state for story apps.

### 5. Australian picture books for under-fives: what the well-known titles do

#### Takeaway
The Australian canon for the very young leans on native animals as characters, a strongly rhythmic or patterned read-aloud text, dry and often deadpan humour, and specific named places (the bush, the beach, a lap around the continent). Mem Fox's stated craft position is that rhythm (not necessarily rhyme) is everything and that a picture-book text has to be tested and edited by reading it aloud until it is perfect.

#### Cited Findings
- **Possum Magic** (Mem Fox, illus. Julie Vivas, 1983): Grandma Poss and Hush; first drafted in 1978, rejected by nine publishers over five years; a publisher asked for it to be shortened and for the mice to become possums. Described as a rhythmical story of Australia's landscapes and animals; Australia's best-selling picture book. Sales figures vary by source: over 3 million, over 3.5 million, or over 5 million copies. — [Wikipedia: Possum Magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possum_Magic); [Wikipedia: Mem Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mem_Fox); [National Portrait Gallery](https://ftp.portrait.gov.au/people/mem-fox-1946)
- Criticism: in 2021 Edith Cowan University researchers criticised over-reliance on classics such as Possum Magic in classrooms because they are not culturally diverse. — [Wikipedia: Possum Magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possum_Magic)
- **Where is the Green Sheep?** (Mem Fox, illus. Judy Horacek, Penguin, 2004): grew from Fox seeing Horacek's etching of a green sheep in 2002; text is a patterned, rhyming-pair chant ("Here is the blue sheep, and here is the red sheep. Here is the bath sheep, and here is the bed sheep. But where is the green sheep?"); won the 2005 CBCA Book of the Year: Early Childhood; still ranked #7 in Better Reading's 2025 Top 50 Kids' Books. — [Mem Fox's site](https://memfox.com/books/where-is-the-green-sheep/); [Judy Horacek's site](https://horacek.com.au/books/where-is-the-green-sheep); [Better Reading](https://www.betterreading.com.au/book/where-is-the-green-sheep/)
- **Diary of a Wombat** (Jackie French, illus. Bruce Whatley, 2002): Mothball is based closely on a real wombat French knew; Whatley painted it in acrylics; the humour comes from a wombat's terse diary voice. — [Wikipedia: Diary of a Wombat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diary_of_a_Wombat); [Wonderground interview with Jackie French](https://wonderground.press/people/writing-wombating-chat-jackie-french/)
- **Wombat Stew** (Marcia K. Vaughan, illus. Pamela Lofts, 1984/85; 40th anniversary edition 2024): a dingo catches a wombat for stew; platypus, emu, blue-tongued lizard, koala and echidna trick him into adding mud, feathers, flies and bugs; the repeated "Wombat stew" chant and rhythmic text are what reviewers credit for its appeal. — [Better Beginnings](https://www.better-beginnings.com.au/book-finder/wombat-stew); [Aussie Reviews](https://aussiereviews.com/2005/12/wombat-stew-by-marcia-k-vaughan-illustrated-by-pamela-lofts/); [Better Reading: 40th anniversary edition](https://www.betterreading.com.au/book/wombat-stew-40th-anniversary-edition/)
- **Koala Lou** (Mem Fox) was illustrated by Pamela Lofts. — [Better Reading: Wombat Stew](https://www.betterreading.com.au/book/wombat-stew-40th-anniversary-edition/)
- **Are We There Yet?** (Alison Lester): a family road trip around Australia, based on Lester's own family trip; won the 2005 CBCA Picture Book of the Year; reviewers single out its colours of place: the red centre, the reef, the "shadowy green rainforest". — [Wikipedia: Alison Lester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alison_Lester); [ReadPlus review](https://readplus.com.au/reviews/are-we-there-yet-by-alison-lester); [Reading Australia](https://readingaustralia.com.au/?p=8864)
- **Magic Beach** (Alison Lester, 1990): based on the real beach (Walkerville South, Victoria) where Lester spent every summer; the text's collective refrain is "at our beach, our magic beach"; originally about one girl, Meg, until an editor suggested broadening it; sold over 500,000 copies; named Guardian Australia's best children's picture book of all time; adapted for film in 2024. — [South Gippsland Sentinel-Times](https://www.sgst.com.au/alison-lester-at-her-beach-when-all-time-childrens-book-named/); [Better Reading](https://www.betterreading.com.au/book/magic-beach-2/); [inkl/Guardian profile](https://www.inkl.com/news/inside-the-idyllic-studio-of-alison-lester-everything-i-do-looks-a-bit-like-a-stuffed-toy)
- Lester grew up in South Gippsland among bush, animals and farmland, and says books about family and her own places (Magic Beach, Are We There Yet?, My Farm) are closest to her. — [Wikipedia: Alison Lester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alison_Lester); [Whispering Gums](https://whisperinggums.com/2020/10/05/monday-musings-on-australian-literature-alison-lester/)
- **Bluey books** (Penguin Random House Australia, from 2019): 1 million sold by June 2020; 5 million across 44 titles by October 2022; over 10 million across 100 titles by October 2025; PRH claims the #1 children's book brand in Australia on Nielsen BookScan; the show is made in Brisbane. — [Books+Publishing](https://www.booksandpublishing.com.au/articles/2020/06/18/152216/sales-of-bluey-books-hit-1-million-copies/); [Penguin: 5 million](https://www.penguin.com.au/news/3700-bluey-books-hit-5-million-sales-in-australia-and-new-zealand-range); [Penguin: 10 million](https://www.penguin.com.au/news/5097-10-million-bluey-books-sold)
- Older foundations: Snugglepot and Cuddlepie (May Gibbs, 1918), Blinky Bill (Dorothy Wall, 1933), The Magic Pudding (Norman Lindsay, 1918), The Muddle-Headed Wombat (Ruth Park, 1962), all long in print. — [Wikipedia: Blinky Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blinky_Bill); [Sydney Expert: Classic Australian Children's Books](https://sydneyexpert.com/classic-australian-childrens-books/)
- **Mem Fox on rhythm and reading aloud** (her own advice pages):
  - "Rhythm needs to be in the marrow of your bones if you're thinking of writing a picture book. I don't mean rhymes, necessarily." — [Mem Fox: Writing Picture Books](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/writing-picture-books)
  - Writers should read every paragraph, sentence and phrase aloud, repeatedly, because the book is written to be read aloud; "almost" right rhythm is never good enough and only reading aloud shows whether it is perfect. — [Mem Fox: Writing Picture Books](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/writing-picture-books)
  - "When I edit, I read aloud, and read aloud, and read aloud. I do it to hear the rightness of the words and the wrongness." — [NAEYC interview with Mem Fox](https://naeyc.org/node/1190)
  - The "so what?" test: is this a so-what story, or one that will be around forever? And: read recent picture books over and over. — [Mem Fox: Writing Do's and Don'ts](https://memfox.squarespace.com/advice/twenty-writing-dos-and-donts)
  - Reported from a talk: "What delights young children? It's the rhythm. It's always the rhythm." (secondhand) — [Christine Knight: Mem Fox on the story behind Possum Magic](https://christineknight.me/2014/11/mem-fox-story-behind-possum-magic-read-kids/)
  - Fox's "Ten Read-Aloud Commandments" for parents exist on her site and are widely reproduced by maternal and child health services. — [Greater Shepparton MCH reproduction](https://greatershepparton.com.au/assets/files/documents/community/childrens/mch/2-year/Maternal_and_Child_Health_Mem_Fox_Commandments_2yr_KAS_Pack.PDF)

#### Inferences
- The recurring devices are: a chantable refrain a two-year-old can join in ("wombat stew", "where is the green sheep?", "our magic beach"); a question the page turn answers; a cast of native animals each with one clear trait; a real, specific place drawn from the author's own life; and understated, slightly cheeky humour where the animal is unimpressed by humans (Diary of a Wombat) or the villain is outwitted, not hurt (Wombat Stew).
- The patterned question-and-reveal structure of Where is the Green Sheep? maps directly onto a digital lift-the-flap: the flap is the answer to the refrain, which is the story-congruent interaction the research in section 2 favours.
- Fox's read-aloud test is the same test a "read together" mode needs: text written for the adult's mouth, tried aloud many times, perfect in rhythm. It also argues for professional narration with rhythm, not flat reading, in "read to me".
- Australianness in these books is carried by setting, fauna, flora, food and idiom, not by flags or slogans. Possum Magic's plot turns on eating Australian foods around the country (see Gaps for the detail).
- The canon is also a caution: it is not culturally diverse, and several classics present "the bush" as an unpeopled backdrop (see section 7).

### 6. How Australian animals are characterised, and existing famous characters by species

#### Takeaway
Koalas and wombats are heavily occupied territory with long-established, trademark-like characters (Blinky Bill; the Muddle-Headed Wombat; Mothball of Diary of a Wombat). Platypus, echidna and quokka have many picture books but, in the sources found, no single dominant Australian picture-book character, which leaves more room. The main risk for a new cast is echoing a famous name, look or personality within the same species.

#### Inferences
- **Stock traits to be aware of** (so an original cast can either avoid or knowingly play against them): koala = cheeky boy in red dungarees (Blinky Bill) or sleepy and cuddly; wombat = stubborn, hungry, digging, deadpan (Mothball) or lovably muddled (Ruth Park); quokka = smiling, friendly, mischievous snack-thief; echidna = shy, spiky, ant-loving, curls into a ball; platypus = shy, odd-one-out, river-dwelling, often a female life-cycle story.

A koala in red overalls or a wombat narrating a deadpan food diary would read as derivative.
- Platypus, echidna and quokka leads are the least crowded; a wombat or koala lead needs a clearly distinct personality, design and voice.
- This is not legal advice; a trademark search on character names is a separate step.

### 7. Respectful practice with Australian places, nature and First Nations content

#### Takeaway
The settled industry position is that First Nations stories, languages, designs, totems and knowledge are Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP) held by communities, not free material; copyright law does not protect it, so protocols do. A non-Indigenous maker writing original animal stories set in Australian landscapes does not need to include First Nations content, but must not borrow Dreaming-style stories, art styles or language without consent, collaboration, attribution and benefit-sharing; where such content is wanted, the route is a genuine partnership led by the relevant community.

#### Cited Findings
- [Industry guidance] The Australian Society of Authors publishes "More Than Words: Writing Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Culture and Copyright in Australia", a practical guide for authors, illustrators and publishers working with First Nations people or their ICIP. The ASA says ICIP is often owned by a community and not an individual, that relying on the Copyright Act to protect it is inadequate, and that authors of any work with First Nations content are expected to understand and follow the protocols. — [Australian Society of Authors: What you need to know about ICIP](https://www.asauthors.org.au/news/what-you-need-to-know-about-indigenous-cultural-and-intellectual-property/)
- [Industry guidance] The ASA also points to Terri Janke's "True Tracks" principles as a guide to ICIP. — [Australian Society of Authors](https://www.asauthors.org.au/news/what-you-need-to-know-about-indigenous-cultural-and-intellectual-property/)
- [Official/industry] The Australia Council's "Writing Cultures: Protocols for Producing Indigenous Australian Literature" is the older national protocol guide for writing; updated national protocols for using First Nations cultural and intellectual property in the arts have since been launched. — [Writing Cultures (hosted by WIPO)](https://www.wipo.int/export/sites/www/tk/en/databases/creative_heritage/docs/atsia_writing_culture.pdf); [WIPO Magazine on Australia's new protocols](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/wipo-magazine/articles/working-ethically-with-indigenous-cultural-and-intellectual-property-australia-launches-new-protocols-55714)
- [Official] AIATSIS (through Aboriginal Studies Press) publishes "Guidelines for the ethical publishing of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander authors and research from those communities"; the core test is that work with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people has been done with their prior and informed consent and benefits them in ways they value; the guidelines cover Aboriginal language on the page, marketing and case studies. — [AIATSIS ethical publishing guidelines](https://aiatsis.gov.au/aboriginal-studies-press/getting-published/ethical-publishing-guidelines)
- [Official] The AIATSIS Code of Ethics for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Research supersedes the 2012 guidelines and sets national standards for all research involving First Nations people. — [AIATSIS Code of Ethics](https://aiatsis.gov.au/research/ethical-research/code-ethics)
- [Publisher] Magabala Books is an Aboriginal-owned and -led publishing house whose mandate is publishing Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander writers, storytellers and illustrators. It will consider collaborative works between Indigenous and non-Indigenous authors and says "we examine the process and relationships behind the collaboration carefully". — [Magabala Books: About us](https://www.magabala.com/about-us); [Magabala collaborative submissions](https://magabalabooks.submittable.com/submit/f026400f-9260-49d4-9ef5-93e7592df62c/collaborative-submissions)
- [Commentary] Anita Heiss and others have set out questions non-Indigenous authors should ask before writing Indigenous characters or stories; a 2024 case (a celebrity author's children's book withdrawn after criticism of its portrayal of a First Nations child, published without consultation) is the standard cautionary example. — [InDaily: First Nations people first: where Jamie Oliver went wrong](https://www.indailysa.com.au/inreview/books-poetry/2024/11/12/first-nations-people-first-where-jamie-oliver-went-wrong); [inkl/The Conversation](https://www.inkl.com/news/want-to-write-the-great-australian-novel-you-need-to-engage-with-indigenous-australia-first)
- [Research commentary] Classics such as Possum Magic have been criticised for a lack of cultural diversity when over-relied on in classrooms. — [Wikipedia: Possum Magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possum_Magic)

#### Inferences
What to avoid (drawn from the protocol documents' principles; the specific list is the researcher's application to animal picture books):
- Retelling, adapting or inventing "Dreamtime-style" origin stories ("how the echidna got its spines") for native animals. These are, or imitate, community-owned stories.
- Imitating First Nations art styles (dot painting, cross-hatching/rarrk, X-ray art) in illustration or UI.
- Using words from First Nations languages as character or place names for flavour, without permission from the language's custodians. (Many common animal names, such as "quokka", "koala" and "wombat", are themselves borrowings from Aboriginal languages; ordinary English use is not the issue, but coining new names from a language list is.)
- Depicting sacred sites, ceremony, or First Nations characters as scenery or as generic "wise elder" figures.
- Presenting the bush as empty, untouched or "discovered".

When to seek collaboration:
- As soon as a story includes a First Nations character, language, story, artwork, or a culturally significant real place: engage the relevant community or a First Nations creator early, with informed consent, shared control over how material is used, attribution, and payment/benefit-sharing (the True Tracks pattern).
- When naming a real place, consider dual names or the Traditional Owners' name only with correct sourcing; an Acknowledgement of Country in the app's parent section is a low-risk, widely used courtesy, ideally specific to where the work was made.
- An original cast of animals in an invented but recognisably Australian setting, with accurate natural history (what the animals eat, where they live, which species share a habitat), sits outside ICIP concerns and is the low-risk path. Accuracy about nature is itself a form of respect: quokkas are from south-west Western Australia and platypuses from eastern Australia and Tasmania, so putting them in one creek is a choice to make knowingly (the ranges are background knowledge, unverified here).
