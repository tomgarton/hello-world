## 1. Bluey: episode structure, how an episode grows from a game, the parents, play, emotional truth, dual audience, Brumm's process, Brisbane

### Takeaway
A Bluey episode is a seven-minute single-plot short film that usually starts from a real game or family conflict Joe Brumm noted down, is driven by the emotions that arise inside the game (jealousy, regret), has no antagonist and no curriculum, and lets any lesson emerge from the play itself. The parents are equal partners who join the game and take their roles seriously while being visibly reluctant or tired, and the setting is a specific, recognisable Brisbane.

### Cited Findings

Format and length
- Running time is 7 minutes; the show was ordered as 52 seven-minute episodes. Exceptions: "The Sign" (Series 3) runs 28 minutes; "Minisodes" run one to three minutes. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- A conflicting figure: one source says the content of each episode is roughly seven minutes and most episodes are nine minutes with credits (via search summary, not read in full). — [The Mary Sue](https://themarysue.com/how-long-are-bluey-episodes-answered)
- Brumm has said he aims to stick to a three-act structure or story circle so that it feels as though a character has been somewhere, and that the writers "rarely have B stories and just condense and condense" until the episode reaches length (via search summary; the summary did not make clear which page carried the quote, most likely the Ragan article). — [Ragan: Lessons on life, writing and storytelling from the creator of Bluey](https://ragan.com/?p=288582)
- Ragan's own summary line says Brumm "shares guidance on finding the concise core of a story". — [Ragan](https://ragan.com/?p=288582)

How an episode grows from a game
- Brumm writes most scripts. His process "sometimes begins with making notes about his family's experiences", including the games his children play and the conflicts between them. He described each episode as "a chance to make a short film". — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Brumm said he started writing down the games his children played, which often devolved into "'Monty Python' bizarre scenarios"; the "Ticklecrabs" game was originally called Pinchy, after a crab he pretended would sneak home from the beach with him (via search summary; NPR page timed out). — [NPR: The creator of Bluey writes for both kids and grown-ups](https://www.npr.org/2022/11/29/1139765338/the-creator-of-bluey-writes-for-both-kids-and-grown-ups)
- Brumm: the best episodes have "wild premises that follow rigid logic", and "Each episode has a number of components to make it good … It's not just about coming up with a funny game." (via search summary; exact page not confirmed, results pointed to the Deadline interview syndicated on Yahoo and to World Screen). — [Deadline via Yahoo](https://malaysia.news.yahoo.com/bluey-creator-joe-brumm-making-131830300.html); [World Screen](https://worldscreen.com/tvkids/?p=22314)
- Bluey and Bingo play during ordinary activities such as a supermarket trip. Daley Pearson (Ludo co-founder, named "Pearson" in the article) said the emotions in play, such as jealousy and regret, drive the conflicts, and that there is no antagonist. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Writer Tim Bain's Bluey episode came from his own memory of riding on his dad's shoulders; he credits Bluey with blending relatable comedy with a discovery that resolves a character's problem. — [Skwigly: Tim Bain Q&A](https://www.skwigly.co.uk/tim-bain-writing-childrens-kids-animation/)

Production process
- "It starts with me writing the episodes. Once we have those, we're all under one roof in the middle of Brisbane at Ludo Studio. There are 50 or 60 of us." (Brumm, via search summary). — [World Screen](https://worldscreen.com/tvkids/?p=22314)
- Script writing takes up to two months after the idea; storyboard artists make 500 to 800 drawings over three weeks; a black-and-white animatic is cut with separately recorded dialogue; animation, backgrounds, design and layout take four weeks; a complete episode takes three to four months and about fifteen are in development at once. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- The whole team watches a near-finished episode on Fridays; these became test screenings with families and friends. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Producer Sam Moor said few writers besides Brumm work on the series, mostly animators already on the team. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Brumm conceived the idea while working on Charlie and Lola, considered Peppa Pig as a model, and cited Peanuts and The Simpsons. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))

Child development and play
- Brumm said watching his two daughters play was "as natural to them as breathing". He wanted to show that unstructured, self-directed play shapes children, and consulted research on socio-dramatic play by Sara Smilansky and Vivian Paley. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- After his daughter struggled with formal schooling he left out literacy and numeracy and focused on life skills; he wanted to depict his experience as a parent and not explicitly teach. "There's no counting in Bluey, there's no learning this or that ... just show 'em playing." (Brumm, 2019). — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Developmental researchers (Molly Scott, Temple; Aria Gastón-Panthaki, Harvard; Douglas Piper, Georgetown) say the show exemplifies the finding that children learn through play, and that pretend play lets children process fears and feelings. Their examples: "Copycat" (Bluey re-enacts finding an injured bird and insists the game end in bad news; Chilli, playing the vet, follows her direction), "Early Baby" (Indy and classmates play out a sibling in neonatal intensive care), "Rain" (Chilli helps build the dam instead of stopping it), "Flatpack" (packaging becomes an imaginary world), "Wagon Ride" (Bandit invents a hand-on-arm signal so Bluey can get his attention without interrupting), "Grannies" and "Phones" (grandparents drawn into play, including by video call). — [The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/bluey-teaches-children-and-parents-alike-about-how-play-supports-creativity/)

No stated moral
- Lessons (following rules, the influence of technology and money) emerge through gameplay; the article describes no stated moral. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))

The parents
- Both parents guide and join in play and validate the children's emotions; critics describe them as equal partners. Bandit is an archaeologist who does housework and plays imaginative games; USA Today's Jennifer McClellan called him "sarcastic, sympathetic and silly" and Chilli the "voice of reason". Chilli works part-time in airport security, and flashbacks show her struggles as a new mother. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Bandit takes his game roles seriously despite often seeming reluctant; his attempts to teach life lessons sometimes strain things with his daughters. Chilli is level-headed, balances the chaos, and joins in when she wants. — [Wikipedia: List of Bluey characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_characters)
- Brumm confirmed Bandit is an extension of himself (via search summary). — [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/bluey-gold-pen-game-inspiration-joe-brumm-2000696293)

Brisbane on screen
- Set in semi-tropical Queensland with Brisbane as the model: Queenslander houses (high-set, verandas), Queen Street Mall, South Bank, The Big Pelican on the Noosa River; jacaranda and poinciana trees; fruit bats, wallabies, kookaburras, ibises; rugby league and State of Origin. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- Regional loyalties are character detail: Lucky supports the Maroons, Mackenzie's parents are All Blacks fans. — [Wikipedia: List of Bluey characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_characters)

### Inferences
- The working template is: ordinary setting (home, car, shop) + a game with a clear rule + an emotional snag inside the game + the game's own logic supplies the resolution. One plot, no B story, no villain.
- "Wild premise, rigid logic" is in effect a world rule: the game's rules are never broken for convenience, which is what lets a seven-minute story feel fair and complete.
- The dual audience is achieved structurally and not by adult jokes laid on top: the child follows the game, the adult follows the parent inside the game (tired, reluctant, committed anyway). Both are watching the same scene.
- The Friday family screenings are a cheap, repeatable test worth copying for book drafts: read aloud to real children and watch where attention drops.

### Gaps
- I could not open a primary Brumm interview in full, so quotes on structure are second-hand. The "rules" below are from background knowledge and are unverified in this session: Brumm has described a rule that episodes are told from the children's point of view and that games must be ones children could really play; the writers' room is small and Brumm wrote almost every episode of Series 1 to 3; Brumm announced in late 2024 that he was stepping back from writing the series to work on the feature film. Check before relying on these.
- No source found tying the seven-minute length to research; it appears to be a broadcaster slot length.
- No script page count found.

## 2. Bluey's characters: how the cast is kept distinct

### Takeaway
Each character is fixed by a small set of markers: an exact age, a breed (which doubles as surname, silhouette and colour), one or two temperament words, a family circumstance, and one recurring behaviour or want. Speech differences are used sparingly but precisely (Muffin's L/R sounds, Socks barking, Jean-Luc's French, Pom Pom's squeak).

### Cited Findings
All from [Wikipedia: List of Bluey characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_characters) unless marked.

- Bluey: six turning seven; Blue Heeler; curious, energetic, imaginative, sometimes impulsive; loves pretend play and role-playing as a grown-up.
- Bingo: four turning five; Red Heeler; sweet, sensitive, shy; interested in bugs; favourite toy a stuffed rabbit called Floppy; at kindy.
- Muffin: three, later four; White Heeler cousin; loud, energetic, can be selfish and tantrum-prone but good-hearted; struggles with "L" and "R" sounds.
- Socks: one, later two; Blue Heeler; Muffin's baby sister; at first barks and walks on all fours, then learns to walk and talk and later forms full sentences. Fan speculation about developmental delay is not confirmed by the makers. — also [Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/bluey-socks-dog-behavior-explained/)
- Bandit and Chilli: see section 1. Chilli's mother died before the series; her father Mort is ex-Army and lively despite age and recent surgery.
- Stripe (Bandit's younger brother): gentle, inexperienced at parenting, friendly rivalry with Bandit. Trixie: caring, level-headed; she and Stripe sometimes argue about parenting and find a middle ground.
- Nana (Chris Heeler): indulgent with granddaughters, though shown as a strict mother in a flashback episode; voiced by Brumm's mother, the character's inspiration. Bob: learned to floss with Nana in "Grannies".
- Mackenzie: Border Collie; moved from New Zealand; big-hearted, likes to get to the point, good at herding; as a puppy was frightened when he thought his mum had left him.
- Rusty: red Australian Kelpie; athletic, tenacious, sometimes stubborn; wants to play cricket for Australia; father in the Army; older brother Digger, younger sister Dusty.

- Chloe: Dalmatian; sweet, smart; Bluey's best friend; has a baby brother.
- Lucky: Labrador; next-door neighbour; energetic, kind, rarely raises his voice; sporty; brother Chucky; dad Pat.
- Honey: Beagle; caring, neat, orderly; sometimes struggles to keep up with Bluey's games; wears round glasses; British parents but an Australian accent.
- Coco: Poodle; sweet but can be controlling and impatient; one of nine children.
- Snickers: Dachshund; self-conscious about his long body and short limbs; likes pretending to be a news reporter.

- Winton: English Bulldog; trouble with personal space, improving; parents divorced.

- Child characters are voiced by children of the production crew and are not credited, for privacy.

Breed and colour as identity
- The family is "modelled after real-life blue and red Australian cattle dogs or heelers"; each supporting character is a different dog breed (Coco a pink Poodle, Chloe a Dalmatian). Bluey and Bingo are girls with the "rough and tumble" traits of real puppies. Brumm based Bluey on his childhood Blue Heeler. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- More than 50 dog breeds appear in the show (via search summary of consumer press; not an official count). — [Romper](https://www.romper.com/entertainment/what-kind-of-dog-is-bluey)
- Colour palette described by Sam Moor as "a vibrant pastel". — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))

### Inferences
- Breed supplies three things at once: a one-glance silhouette and colour, a surname, and a temperament hint (herding Border Collie, tireless Kelpie, tidy Beagle). For a non-animal cast the equivalent is a signature colour, shape and one object.
- Blue-for-a-girl and orange-for-a-girl in the lead family means colour marks the individual and the family line and not gender.
- Each friend is given a home circumstance that generates stories without being a "topic" (army dad, new baby, diet, moved country, divorce, shy).
- Ages are spaced: 1, 3, 4, 6. Each age is a different developmental stage, so each child wants and can do different things in the same game. That spacing does a lot of the characterisation work.
- Flaws are age-true and not moral failings (Muffin's tantrums are what three looks like).

- Official Ludo/BBC character descriptions (bluey.tv) were not fetched; temperament words above are Wikipedia's, which may paraphrase them.
- No source found in which the makers explain the breed-and-colour system as a deliberate design rule.
- Characters section of the Wikipedia page beyond 100,000 characters was not read (minor characters).

## 3. Bluey books: formats, how an episode becomes a book, counts, publisher, sales

### Takeaway
Penguin Random House Australia has published Bluey books since November 2019 and reports over 10 million sold across 100 titles in Australia and New Zealand. Episode adaptations come mainly as 24-page board books (often with a novelty such as flaps or glow-in-the-dark) and 32-page hardback picture books that keep the episode's title; activity formats run alongside.

### Cited Findings
- April 2019: BBC Studios partnered with Penguin Random House Australia. First three titles on 5 November 2019: The Beach, Fruit Bat, and the sticker book Time to Play!. All three topped the Australian weekly charts that month and sold 350,000 combined by January 2020. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))
- 1 million copies by June 2020. Wikipedia says this was the first nine books; Books+Publishing's report says seven books (conflict, via search summary for the latter). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series)); [Books+Publishing](https://www.booksandpublishing.com.au/articles/2020/06/18/152216/sales-of-bluey-books-hit-1-million-copies/)
- 5 million by October 2022 across 44 titles (via search summary). — [Penguin Australia news](https://www.penguin.com.au/news/3700-bluey-books-hit-5-million-sales-in-australia-and-new-zealand-range)
- 8 million by November 2024, the fifth anniversary (via search summary). — [Penguin Australia news](https://penguin.com.au/news/4772-penguin-random-house-australia-celebrates-5-years-of-bluey-books)
- 29 October 2025: over 10 million sold across 100 titles in Australia and New Zealand; 48 weeks with a Bluey book at number one in Nielsen BookScan's Australian chart; 342 top-ten appearances; Time to Play! spent 28 weeks on the chart. Formats named: board books, picture books, chapter books, sticker activity, colouring, craft, magnet and sound books, cookbooks, puzzle books, glow-in-the-dark and lenticular pages. The 100th title is an Advent calendar of 24 mini books. Forthcoming: a Keepy Uppy slide-and-play board book and a deluxe comic-style hardback of The Sign. — [Penguin Australia news](https://www.penguin.com.au/news/5097-10-million-bluey-books-sold)
- September 2020: Penguin Random House became master publishing partner with global rights including the US and UK. — [Penguin Australia news](https://www.penguin.com.au/news/5097-10-million-bluey-books-sold)
- The Beach won the 2020 Australian Book Industry Awards Book of the Year, the first picture book to do so. — [Penguin Australia news](https://www.penguin.com.au/news/5097-10-million-bluey-books-sold)
- Jessica Magro of Ludo Studio: "It's a real pleasure bringing Bluey to life in print form." Ludo is described as a publishing partner. — [Penguin Australia news](https://www.penguin.com.au/news/5097-10-million-bluey-books-sold)
- Format breakdown of 79 listed titles: 20 episodic board books (e.g. Fruit Bat, 24 pp, glow-in-the-dark; The Beach, 24 pp, lift-the-flap); 17 episodic hardbacks (e.g. Verandah Santa, 32 pp; Sleepytime, 32 pp, fold-out pages); 3 episodic chapter paperbacks (Trains, Bus, Taxi, 80 pp each, 2024); 6 other board books (All About Bluey, 12 pp, shaped); 13 other hardbacks (My Dad is Awesome, 48 pp; Treasury, 160 pp); 20 other paperbacks (Time to Play!, 16 pp sticker; Big Backyard, 64 pp colouring). Three episodic board books are lift-the-flap (The Beach, Charades, Hooray, It's Easter!). — [Wikipedia: List of Bluey books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_books)
- Episodic books take the episode's title; the one exception is Hooray, It's Easter! (episode "Easter"). — [Wikipedia: List of Bluey books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Bluey_books)

### Inferences
- The house format is 24 pages for an episode-as-board-book and 32 for an episode-as-picture-book. A seven-minute episode therefore becomes roughly 10 to 14 spreads, which implies one story beat per spread and the removal of most dialogue exchanges and running gags.
- The first three launch titles show the range strategy: one quiet story, one bedtime story with a novelty finish, one activity book.
- Non-episodic titles are built around a relationship (My Dad is Awesome) or a character primer (All About Bluey, 12 pp shaped board), which is the book equivalent of a character profile.

### Gaps
- No stated word counts found for any Bluey book. No statement from an editor or from Ludo on how an episode is cut down (who adapts, what is dropped). The inference above about beats per spread is mine.

- No global sales figure found; all milestones are Australia and New Zealand only.
- Wikipedia's list (79) is behind PRH's count (100).

## 4. Other properties: Peppa Pig, Hey Duggee, Daniel Tiger, Maisy, Sarah & Duck

### Takeaway
All five keep stories to one small, concrete, everyday event in a fixed container (five, seven or eleven minutes), and four of the five use a narrator to do the work that young characters cannot do in dialogue. Daniel Tiger is the only one built on an explicit curriculum, delivered as a repeated sung strategy.

### Cited Findings

Peppa Pig
- Episodes are about five minutes and show everyday activities: playgroup, games, visiting relatives, the playground. Most end with the characters falling on their backs laughing. — [Wikipedia: Peppa Pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peppa_Pig)
- The narrator (John Sparkes) reinforces the action and humour and usually says "Oh, dear" when something goes wrong, such as George crying. — [Wikipedia: Peppa Pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peppa_Pig)
- Each friend is a different animal species; characters make their species' noise while talking, wear clothes and live in houses. — [Wikipedia: Peppa Pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peppa_Pig)
- A scriptwriting teacher uses it as an example of a simple three-act structure inside five minutes; episodes start from an issue typical of a young child, such as being scared by thunder (via search summary; single educator's view). — [Wikipedia: Peppa Pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peppa_Pig)
- Created by Neville Astley and Mark Baker, produced by Phil Davies (Astley Baker Davies). — [Wikipedia: Peppa Pig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peppa_Pig)
- Critics contrast Bandit with Daddy Pig, calling Bandit more emotionally intelligent. — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))

Hey Duggee
- Grant Orchard on the set-up: "There's a carer, a kind of club environment, and a few kids", "held together by this very traditional preschool dynamic"; he kept the frame conventional so the content inside could vary, and wanted to "set a precedent right from the start that anything goes". Six main characters are often in one shot. Scriptwriters work off-site. — [Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/?p=149069)
- Core pattern: a problem or interest appears, Duggee turns out to have a badge for it, the Squirrels earn that badge, and the episode closes with the "Duggee Hug" before the children go home to their parents (via search summary; Guardian piece not opened, 403). — [The Guardian via inkl](https://inkl.com/news/we-got-so-many-things-wrong-how-we-made-hey-duggee); [Wikipedia: List of Hey Duggee episodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Hey_Duggee_episodes)
- Episodes are titled "The [X] Badge"; writers besides Orchard include James Henry, Sam Morrison, Myles McLeod, Jo Clegg and Danny Stack. — [Wikipedia: List of Hey Duggee episodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Hey_Duggee_episodes)

Daniel Tiger's Neighborhood
- Created by Angela Santomero, premiered on PBS Kids in 2012; an animated musical series. Target age given as 2 to 4 by Fred Rogers Productions and 2 to 5 elsewhere. — [Fred Rogers Productions](https://www.fredrogers.org/production/daniel-tigers-neighborhood/); [Wikipedia: Daniel Tiger's Neighborhood](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Tiger)
- Each episode is two 11-minute stories that play out in real time, paced so preschoolers can follow. Daniel is four and "invites young viewers into his world, giving them a kid's-eye view of his life." Each story was written with input from early-learning specialists and tested in formative research with children. — [Fred Rogers Productions](https://www.fredrogers.org/production/daniel-tigers-neighborhood/)
- Each theme is reinforced by a short musical phrase, the "strategy song"; "Preschoolers and parents report singing—and using—the strategy songs together in their daily lives." Learning goals listed: empathy, imagination, respect, school readiness, self-control and emotional regulation, self-esteem, social skills. — [Fred Rogers Productions](https://www.fredrogers.org/production/daniel-tigers-neighborhood/)
- PBS describes the show as built on Fred Rogers' social-emotional curriculum plus strategy songs. — [PBS Kids: Life's Little Lessons](https://pbskids.org/learn/lifes-little-lessons)
- Santomero said the Fred Rogers Company supplied the socio-emotional curriculum Rogers refined over 40 years and all his music; her team meets the company to choose themes, works out the "Fred-ish" way to handle each, then builds the story; the adult characters model the strategies Rogers would have modelled. Joanne Rogers: "Daniel was the real Fred." (via search summary). — [HuffPost](https://www.huffingtonpost.ca/entry/fred-rogers-daniel-tigers-neighborhood_us_5b1fe6cee4b09d7a3d777355)
- Example strategy song title: "When You Feel So Mad That You Want to Roar". — [TEKS Guide resource](https://teksguide.org/resource/when-you-feel-so-mad-you-want-roar-strategy-song-and-activity-daniel-tigers-neighborhood)

Maisy
- Lucy Cousins' books have been published by Walker Books in the UK since 1990 and by Candlewick in the US; a TV series was made in 1999 and a later series, My Friend Maisy, is based on the books. — [Wikipedia: Maisy (book series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maisy_(book_series))
- Sales figures differ by source: over 26 million (Walker pages), over 28 million (Wikipedia), and one bookseller page claiming 46 million (via search summary; treat 26 to 28 million as the consistent range). — [Walker Books Australia](https://walkerbooks.com.au/book/9781406329513); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maisy_(book_series))
- Titles show the story unit: one first experience or outing per book (Maisy Goes to Bed, Maisy Goes Shopping, Maisy Goes to the Library, Maisy Goes on Holiday, Maisy at the Fair), plus flap and word books. — [Walker Books Australia](https://www.walkerbooks.com.au/book/9780763615031/)

Sarah & Duck
- Created by Sarah Gomes Harris and Tim O'Sullivan. The narrator (Roger Allam) talks directly to Sarah and Duck, asks questions and makes suggestions; Sarah answers him. — [Wikipedia: Sarah & Duck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_%26_Duck)
- Producer Jamie Badminton said Allam's dry humour never contradicts the sincerity of his conversations with Sarah and Duck (via search summary). — [all-allam.com](https://all-allam.com/?p=1456)
- Duck does not speak; he quacks and acts (general description, via search summary). — [TV Tropes: Sarah & Duck](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/WesternAnimation/SarahAndDuck)

### Inferences
- Three narrator models: commentator outside the story (Peppa, "Oh, dear"); host who frames and jokes (Hey Duggee); conversational companion the child answers (Sarah & Duck). Daniel Tiger has no narrator; the child addresses the viewer directly. Bluey has no narrator at all, which is part of why it needs older, more verbal leads.
- A narrator is what lets very young or non-speaking characters carry a story. For a toddler lead and a baby, a narrator (or a picture-book text voice) is the obvious tool.
- Each show has a fixed ritual ending (laughing on backs, the Duggee Hug, the strategy song reprise). For a book series a fixed last spread does the same job.
- The Maisy model (one first experience per book, named in the title) is the smallest viable story unit for under-threes; the Bluey model (a game with an emotional snag) suits three to five.

### Gaps
- Maisy's text style (present tense, short declarative sentences, a narrator who addresses Maisy and the reader in the TV series) is from background knowledge; no source confirmed it in this session.
- Hey Duggee: narrator is Alexander Armstrong per a Wikipedia mirror only; the seven-minute length, Duggee speaking only in "woof", the narrator interpreting him, and the Squirrels' names and traits (Roly, Tag, Betty, Happy, Norrie) were not sourced in this session.
- Peppa Pig: ages (Peppa four, George two), George's one word "dinosaur" and his crying, alliterative species names (Suzy Sheep, Rebecca Rabbit), the grandparents' boat and garden, and Ladybird as book publisher are background knowledge, not confirmed here.
- Whether both Daniel Tiger stories in an episode share one strategy song (I believe they do) was not confirmed by the Fred Rogers Productions page.
- No direct interview found with the Peppa or Sarah & Duck creators on writing rules.

## 5. Series bibles and character profiles for preschool

### Takeaway
Industry guidance agrees on four parts: world, characters, stories, format. Character entries cover background, goals, emotional tone, look, speech and above all relationships. I found no published preschool-specific template and no source that uses the term "story engine"; the nearest documented equivalents are the "episode structure" page and the "rules of the world" page.

### Cited Findings
- Jeffrey Scott (700+ animation scripts) names four vital elements: the World (setting, key locations, recurring objects), the Characters, the Stories, the Format. Characters: "backgrounds, their goals and motivations, their emotional tone, their look and dress", plus relationships. Stories: "a half dozen to an entire season's worth of story ideas", each a short beginning, middle and end showing how the elements fit. Format: what the show is about, theme, genre, episode length, style, role of music, target age. For Muppet Babies the nursery's furniture and toys were chosen because they let the characters play out stories, and "The theme of the show was imagination." — [AnimationXpress: The four vital elements of an animated series bible](https://www.animationxpress.com/latest-news/guest-column-htwfa-31-the-four-vital-elements-of-an-animated-series-bible/)
- A pitch bible typically has cover with title and key image, logline of one or two sentences, synopsis, characters (only those in most episodes), world, storylines and episode structure, at about 20 pages and no more than 40. A writers' bible adds character relationships, location detail, detailed episode structure and curriculum (via search summary). — [Prime the Animation: Animated TV series bibles (PDF)](https://primetheanimation.upv.es/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/AnimatedTVSeriesBibles_PrimetheAnimation2020.pdf)
- One creator's order: logline, concept page, per-episode structure page, rules of the world; in animation anything goes as long as the rules stay consistent. State target age on the cover, splitting preschool into 1 to 3 and 3 to 5; put children front and centre with adults in support (via search summary). — [Pick Up Your Pen: Developing a kids' animation series, part two](https://pickupyourpen.com/developing-a-kids-animated-tv-series-part-2/)
- Character bibles should include speech patterns and vocabulary, contradictions, and how the character responds under pressure (via search summary; a studio blog, lower authority). — [Whizzy Studios](https://www.whizzystudios.com/post/how-to-write-a-character-bible-for-kids-animation)
- Tim Bain (writer on Bluey and other preschool shows): "Write a bible, look at other bibles if you can"; preschool writing means "focusing on a character's inner most emotions" with "a lot of heart and empathy"; children swing "between different emotions from one second to the next"; adults "definitely take a back seat"; stories follow children's own goals such as "seeking fun or making friendships"; read dialogue aloud. — [Skwigly](https://www.skwigly.co.uk/tim-bain-writing-childrens-kids-animation/)
- General TV guidance: recurring characters should have their own wants and problems (via search summary). — [Final Draft](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/10-steps-to-developing-your-tv-show-bible)
- Kidscreen Summit has run a mentor class titled "Building bibles that work", but the listing gives no content. — [Kidscreen Summit](https://summit.kidscreen.com/2018/sessions/68752/soldoutmentorclass)
- Daniel Tiger shows what a curriculum statement looks like in practice: a named list of learning goals, one theme per story, and one repeatable strategy. — [Fred Rogers Productions](https://www.fredrogers.org/production/daniel-tigers-neighborhood/)
- Bluey shows the opposite values statement: no numeracy or literacy, life skills only, "just show 'em playing". — [Wikipedia: Bluey (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluey_(TV_series))

### Inferences
- A workable profile template, assembled from the sources above and from how Bluey's cast is actually differentiated: name; exact age; one-line essence; look (colour, shape, signature object); want; fear or soft spot; age-true flaw; recurring behaviour; how they speak (vocabulary size, catchphrase, sound); home and family circumstance; relationship to each other regular; how they behave under pressure; what they never do. The "never does" line and the "fear" line are common practice in my experience but were not found stated in a source here.
- "Story engine" can be written as one sentence that generates episodes, for example Hey Duggee's "the Squirrels meet something new and earn the badge for it" or Bluey's "an ordinary family moment becomes a game, and the game teaches someone something". A bible should then list ten or more springboards that prove the engine runs.
- "World rules" for preschool usually cover: whose point of view; whether adults can solve the problem (normally not); whether imagination is shown as real on the page; what never appears (villains, screens, peril); how every story ends.
- A values or curriculum statement should say what the series is for and also what it deliberately does not teach.

## 6. Writing a baby and a toddler who barely speak; giving parents and grandparents a real place

### Takeaway
The successful examples give the pre-verbal child a physical repertoire, one sound or word, and a clear want, and let an older sibling, parent or narrator interpret. Adults earn their place by joining the child's game on the child's terms and by having small visible feelings of their own, while the child's goal drives the story.

### Inferences
- Baby profile: list what the baby can do at this exact age (grab, crawl, point, drop, laugh), one signature sound, one thing she always wants (her sister's things, a particular object), and who interprets her. The baby works best as a source of interruptions and accidents that redirect the toddler's game, and as someone the toddler has feelings about.
- Toddler lead profile: a handful of real words and phrases used consistently, big fast-changing feelings, a strong will, and action carried by the pictures. A narrator or the book's text voice speaks around her and not for her.
- Letting the non-verbal character develop over the series (Socks) rewards repeat readers and matches the audience's own growth.
- Two adult models to choose between or mix: the Bluey parent who is a player inside the game with their own small arc, and the Daniel Tiger parent who supplies a strategy. In both, the adult does not solve the problem for the child. Parents can be given one honest feeling per story (tired, distracted, wanting to finish a cup of tea) without taking the plot.
- Grandparents work well as the ones who are taught by the child, who have more time and looser rules than parents, and who carry a different pace and a different house.
